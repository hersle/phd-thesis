\chapter{Background}
\label{chap:back}

Modern cosmology is largely a computational science.

%When Edwin Hubble measured the distances to ... and fit it to $v=H_0 d$,
%he could do the calculation with pen/paper .

Today, the increasing data volume and precision of modern observations makes fully analytical modeling infeasible.
Analytical work is used to formulate the laws of physics and the fundamental equations of cosmology,
such as the Einstein and Boltzmann equations.
However, they take the form of partial differential equations (PDEs) or ordinary differential equations (ODEs)
that are impossible to solve analytically.
Instead, we must now obtain numerical solutions of the fundamental equations through computer simulations.
This involves development and use of advanced numerical methods and software packages that are fast and robust.
In this way, computational science is the bridge that connects the
theoretical and observational frontiers of cosmological research.

Simultaneously, the current standard model of cosmology
-- the \LCDM{} model --
is put under increased scrutiny from the tighter data error bars.
This has revealed inconsistencies where different observational probes
return different constraints for the parameters of the model.
Assuming the data analysis is correct,
this means that the \LCDM{} model is not realistic enough.
Thus, a large fraction of contemporary cosmological research
is devoted to exploring modifications to the standard model.

What does this mean for our computational tools?
It means that not only \enquote{hard} aspects like speed and robustness are priorities,
but so are \enquote{softer} principles such as user-friendliness and flexibility,
in order to facilitate easy implementation of modified cosmological models.
Moreover, we should strive to avoid model-specific approximation schemes and assumptions,
as they make it harder to implement modifications and are a source of error for modifications.

\section{The standard cosmological model}
\label{sec:LCDM}

The \LCDM{} model is the current iteration of the standard model in cosmology
because it is the best explanation we have so far of several observations we have made in the Universe.
It assumes that \cite[e.g.][]{perivolaropoulosChallengesCDMUpdate2022b}:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{\GR{}}
is the correct theory of gravity.
It describes how the geometry of spacetime in the Einstein tensor $G_{\mu\nu}$
reacts to the content of energy and momentum $T_{\mu\nu}$
through the Einstein equations:
\begin{equation}
    G_{\mu\nu} + \Lambda g_{\mu\nu} = 8\pi T_{\mu\nu}.
\label{eq:einstein_intro}
\end{equation}
It also describes the reverse process:
how energy and momentum react to the geometry of spacetime through the geodesic equation:
\begin{equation}
\odv[order=2]{x^\mu}{\lambda} + \Gamma^\mu_{\alpha\beta} \odv{x^\alpha}{\lambda} \odv{x^\beta}{\lambda} = 0 .
\label{eq:geodesic}
\end{equation}

\item \textbf{The cosmological principle}
asserts that the universe is almost homogeneous and isotropic.
As a zeroth order approximation, it is described by the flat
Friedmann--Lemaître--Robertson--Walker (FLRW) spacetime metric:
\begin{equation}
\diff{s}^2 = -\diff{t}^2 + a(t)^2 \left( \diff{r}^2 + r^2 \diff{\theta}^2 + r^2 \sin^2 \theta \, \diff{\phi}^2 \right).
\label{eq:flrw}
\end{equation}
When this $g_{\mu\nu}$ is inserted into the Einstein equation \eqref{eq:einstein_intro}
and one assumes that $T_{\mu\nu}$ is described by a perfect fluid with energy density $\rho$ and pressure $P$,
one obtains the first and second Friedmann equations:
\begin{subequations}
\begin{align}
    H^2 = \left( \frac{\dot{a}}{a} \right)^2 &= \frac{8\pi}{3} \rho + \frac{\Lambda}{3}, \label{eq:fried1} \\
    \dot{H} + H^2 = \frac{\ddot{a}}{a} &= - \frac{4\pi}{3} (\rho + 3P) + \frac{\Lambda}{3}. \label{eq:fried2}
\end{align}%
\label{eq:fried}%
\end{subequations}
They describe how the universe expands with the scale factor $a(t)$ due to $\rho$ and $P$,
which is sourced by the following \enquote{ingredients}.
\TODO{figure of LSS survey?}

\item \textbf{Dark energy}
as a cosmological constant $\Lambda$ was first added by Einstein in his equation \eqref{eq:einstein_intro}
in order for them to give a stable solution for a static (non-expanding) universe, which was assumed at the time.
When Hubble observed that nearby galaxies are recessing and showed that the universe expands with time,
Einstein famously erased it and called it his \enquote{biggest blunder}.
However, it is now back in the equations to explain the acceleration \eqref{eq:fried2}
of the expansion that has been found from supernova observations in the 1990s.
We move $\Lambda$ to the right side of the Einstein equations $G_{\mu\nu} = 8\pi T_{\mu\nu}$,
and absorb it into $T_{\mu\nu}$ with $P = -\rho$.

\item \textbf{Cold dark matter} (CDM)
is an invisible and non-interacting form of matter
that makes up around $25\%$ of the energy density today.
It is observed indirectly through its enhanced effect on the clustering of large-scale structure,
galaxy rotation curves, gravitational lensing and the cosmic microwave background, for example.
It was first suggested (independently?) by Lord Kelvin, Jacobus Kapteyn and Fritz Zwicky,
that the universe contains more matter than the luminous matter we can observe from Earth.
\TODO{cite}

\item \textbf{Baryons}
are \enquote{normal} visible matter that interacts with light,
and makes up around $5\%$ of the energy density today.
\enquote{Baryons} in cosmology includes baryons, such as protons and neutrons, that make up the elements;
but also leptons, such as electrons, which are not baryons, but nevertheless referred to as such by cosmologists.
Baryons cluster like CDM, but unlike CDM the interacting baryon-photon plasma in the early universe
creates outwards pressure that counteract collapse.
The resulting baryon acoustic oscillations (BAO) leaves a detectable signature on the matter distribution and cosmic microwave background.

\item \textbf{Photons}
are massless and thus relativistic particles.
They interact with baryons through scattering off charged particles.
In the early universe the temperature was so high that atoms were ionized and photons constantly scattered off electrons,
but as the universe expanded and became cooler the electrons and ions combined and the universe became transparent to photons.
Photons that were last scattered at this time have since traveled unimpeded through the universe and are observable as the cosmic microwave background today,
which functions us a direct probe of early universe physics.
This was first detected by Penzias and Wilson in 1964,
and has now been refined with the Planck experiment.
They made up a significant fraction of the energy budget in the early universe, but are negligible today.

\item \textbf{Neutrinos}
are massive but light particles in the standard model of particle physics.
They behave relativistically (similar to photons) in the early universe,
but nonrelativistically (similar to matter) in the late universe
as the expansion decreases their momentum.
They decoupled from the primordial plasma in the very early universe,
where they interacted only weakly with electrons and positrons.

\item \textbf{Inflation}
is a brief period of exponentially fast expansion that took place in the very early universe.
Through quantum fluctuations, this created the initial perturbation seeds
that were later amplified by gravity into inhomogeneities.
\end{itemize}

The \LCDM{} model has developed gradually,
and is the best model we have come up with so far
that simultaneously explains
the acceleration of the universe,
anisotropies in the cosmic microwave background
and statistics of large-scale structure
with remarkable success.
However, as gradually uncovered by increasingly precise observations,
it also faces a number of challenges.
For example
\cite{bullLCDMProblemsSolutions2016,abdallaCosmologyIntertwinedReview2022a,perivolaropoulosChallengesCDMUpdate2022b}:
\begin{itemize}
\item
We do not understand the theoretical nature of dark matter and dark energy (the cosmological constant).
The equations of the \LCDM{} model describe only \emph{how} dark matter effectively behaves,
but not what it \emph{is} in terms of physical theories we already understand.
A natural theoretical candidate for the cosmological constant is the vacuum energy density from quantum field theory,
but calculations show that it is off by at least 60 orders of magnitude
\cite[e.g.][]{cirelliDarkMatter2025,weinbergCosmologicalConstantProblem1989,perivolaropoulosChallengesCDMUpdate2022b}.

\item
Observations of the CMB (which depends mostly on early-universe physics)
and of supernova distances (which depend mostly on late-universe physics)
give incompatible values of the Hubble parameter $H_0 = H(t_0)$ today.
This is called the \enquote{Hubble tension} and illustrated in \TODO{figure}.
A similar tension exists for the amplitude of matter clustering from CMB versus LSS data,
but is much less severe
\cite{perivolaropoulosChallengesCDMUpdate2022b}.

\item
We observe a dipole moment in the CMB due to the Solar System's relative motion to it,
but the dipole effect found in the LSS for the same reason is found to be twice as large.
This potential anisotropy in our universe challenges the cosmological principle,
which is a very fundamental assumption in both the \LCDM{} model and common extensions of it
\cite{secrestTestCosmologicalPrinciple2021}.

\item
Recent measurements from the DESI survey of the BAO feature imprinted in the LSS
prefers time-varying models of dark energy when its equation of state is
parameterized as a first-order Taylor expansion in the scale factor around its value today.
\cite{collaborationDESI2024VI2025,collaborationDESIDR2Results2025}

\item
The DESI results also constrain the total neutrino mass by an \emph{upper} bound
that is in tension with the \emph{lower} bound found from terrestrial neutrino oscillation experiments.
However, when the time-varying dark energy model is used instead of the \LCDM{} model,
the constraints become compatible.
\cite{collaborationDESI2024VI2025,collaborationDESIDR2Results2025}

\end{itemize}
These and other discrepancies motivate research on modifying all parts of the cosmological model.
We will consider some examples of modified models in \cref{sec:modifications}.
Every such modification must be implemented into the software
that we use for modifying the cosmological model.

\section{The computational tools of cosmology}

To make predictions from the \LCDM{} model and its extensions,
we must solve their equations of motion numerically with computers.
Here I summarize some of the most important categories of modeling tools in the computational toolbox of cosmology
that are related to my contributions in this thesis.

\subsection{\EB{} codes}

\EB{} codes solve the background and linear perturbations of the coupled \EB{} equations in cosmology.
They describe the Einstein equations \eqref{eq:einstein_intro} coupled to the evolution of the matter content in the universe given by the Boltzmann equation,
and are solved perturbatively around the FLRW metric \eqref{eq:flrw} for a given cosmological model.
These equations must be modified depending on the cosmological model.
The solutions of these equations are used to predict distance measures,
power spectra for the spatial distribution of matter
and power spectra for the angular distribution of anisotropies in the cosmic microwave background
and how this is influenced by gravitational lensing.

%but on shorter scales one must resort to higher-order (and more complicated) perturbation theory or nonlinear simulations.

This is the most fundamental piece of software used for cosmological modeling.
Not only do \EB{} codes lead directly to the important observables mentioned above,
but they also provide initial conditions for other modeling tools such as $N$-body simulations (see below).
The two most established codes today are CAMB \cite{lewisEfficientComputationCMB2000a} and CLASS \cite{lesgourguesCosmicLinearAnisotropy2011d,blasCosmicLinearAnisotropy2011c},
which are directly integrated into parameter estimation tools using Bayesian inference
such as CosmoMC \cite{lewisCosmologicalParametersCMB2002,lewisEfficientSamplingFast2013},
MontePython \cite{audrenConservativeConstraintsEarly2013,brinckmannMontePython3Boosted2018}
and Cobaya \cite{torradoCobayaCodeBayesian2021}.
It is the combination of these tools with observational data
that is responsible for most constraints on cosmological parameters in the contemporary literature.

The codes incorporate a wide range of numerical methods
in order to solve several challenging numerical problems.
This includes large and highly stiff ordinary differential equations,
numerical quadrature of thousands of line-of-sight integrals,
computation of special functions such as (hyper)spherical Bessel functions,
interpolation schemes and parallelization techniques.
Despite these challenges, the codes have been highly refined and run on the order of seconds on a modern laptop.

\Cref{chap:lin} in this thesis describes the largest contribution of my work,
which is the implementation of a new \EB{} code called SymBoltz
\cite{sletmoenSymBoltzjlSymbolicnumericApproximationfree2026}.
It is designed to make it easier to implement modified cosmological models,
remove the need for approximation schemes that complicate the equations in other codes,
and to support modern numerical methods such as automatic differentiation and spectral interpolation methods.

\subsection{Nonlinear \texorpdfstring{$\boldsymbol{N}$}{N}-body simulation codes}

At sufficiently small distance scales and late times,
the linear perturbation theory that underlies \EB{} codes is no longer sufficient.
Here nonlinear effects from gravitational clustering becomes important.
$N$-body codes are codes that simulate the nonlinear evolution of gravitational clustering
by evolving $N$ matter particles in a box that interact with each other due to gravity.
This is extremely expensive as $N$ grows and can take anywhere from minutes to months,
and $N$-body simulation codes can be grouped in two categories.

\enquote{Slow-but-accurate} codes aim to describe the nonlinear clustering as accurately as possible.
This includes both taking physical effects such as baryons into account,
which requires more complicated hydrodynamics solvers,
and using computational techniques such as adaptive mesh refinement in order to
increase the resolution of the computational grid in areas where the particles cluster
to get accurate results down to small scales.
This is good for running one or a few simulations,
but is very demanding for exploring the parameter space of the model.
Some examples of codes in this category are
GADGET \cite{springelGADGETCodeCollisionless2001},
RAMSES \cite{teyssierCosmologicalHydrodynamicsAdaptive2002a} and
AREPO \cite{weinbergerArepoPublicCode2020}.

\enquote{Fast-but-approximate} codes sacrifice accuracy in order to achieve faster computational speeds.
These codes usually neglect baryonic effects and consider only cold dark matter, which results in a simpler and elegant computational problem,
and a fixed particle-mesh (PM) structure of the computational grid.
Some codes employ approximations such as the COLA method \cite{tassevSolvingLargeScale2013b},
which sacrifices accuracy at the very smallest scales in order to take much fewer time steps.
Such methods are more appropriate for exploring dependence throughout parameter space,
and their accuracy sacrifice is less of a downside in applications that study difference or ratios of an observable in two different universes.
Some example of codes in this category are
L-PICOLA \cite{howlettLPICOLAParallelCode2015},
its modified gravity generalization MG-PICOLA \cite{wintherCOLAScaledependentGrowth2017}
that is now succeeded by
FML/COLASolver\footnote{\url{https://github.com/HAWinther/FML/tree/master/FML/COLASolver}},
FastPM \cite{fengFastPMNewScheme2016} and
PySCo \cite{bretonPySCoFastParticleMesh2025}.

\Cref{chap:nonlin} in this thesis describes a smaller fraction of my work in this direction.
There we use \enquote{fast-but-approximate} $N$-body simulations with the COLA method
in order to predict the nonlinear matter power spectrum in modified Brans-Dicke gravity relative to \GR{}.
We show a way to reduce the nonlinear prediction to that from linear \EB{} codes.
This technique can be used in conjunction with emulators (see below) of \GR{} simulations,
in order to make cheap predictions of the nonlinear \BD{} matter power spectrum.

\subsection{Emulators and interpolation methods}

\EB{} and, to a much greater extent, $N$-body simulations are expensive to run.
To estimate cosmological parameters using Bayesian inference,
Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) methods need to sample $10^4$-$10^6$ points in parameter space and in principle run these codes for every sample.
It is therefore very common to \emph{emulate} the output of expensive computations.

Generally speaking, an emulator refers to \emph{any} method that reproduces or \enquote{imitates} the output of a physically principled and expensive function $\mathcal{O}(\mathbf{p})$ of parameters $\mathbf{p}$ using a cheap surrogate model:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{p} \,\,\,\to\,\,\, \mathrm{???} \,\,\,\to\,\,\, \mathcal{O} .
\end{equation}
\emph{How} an emulator works internally (???) is of secondary importance.
The most important point is that the result $\mathcal{O}$ correctly matches the target function within some given tolerance,
and that the evaluation (???) is as fast as possible using whatever means necessary.

Perhaps pedantically and philosophically, how can we be sure that a \enquote{black box} produces correct results when we have no idea what it does?
And how do we reconcile this with our standards for validation, transparency and explainable methods in research?
The most crucial insight is in my opinion the very simple property that observables in our own universe and physical models are \emph{smooth} functions of the parameters.
As one perturbs the parameters $\mathbf{p}$ slightly,
any well-defined observable function that we can detect in our universe should respond smoothly to it,
so that the value of $\mathcal{O}$ and all of its derivatives (which are really just derived observables from $\mathcal{O}$) are continuous functions of $\mathbf{P}$.
This is a fundamental philosophical assumption at least in classical (non-quantum) theories of physics,
and is what makes it possible to describe physics in the mathematical language of calculus and differential equations.

Emulation therefore simply reduces to the mathematical field of function approximation,
which is an extremely wide discipline in mathematics \cite[e.g.][]{trefethenApproximationTheoryApproximation2019}.
%It only happens to be the case that the
%operations inside the smooth function
%-- i.e. an \EB{} codes or $N$-body simulations --
%is long and of physical nature,
%which makes the use of emulators
The expensive function is typically evaluated for a small number of sample points,
and then interpolation and regression methods are used to evaluate it cheaply at other points.
A downside is that this training process must be repeated for every new cosmological model.
Another challenge of traditional interpolation methods is that their cost scales exponentially with the dimension of the parameter space,
but recent explosion of research in artificial intelligence and machine learning have made it feasible to emulate functions of
$O(10)$ cosmological parameters using neural networks, for example.
In this field, the extent to which a function is smooth
is a critical assumption that enables better interpolation methods.

A very large number of emulators and emulator frameworks have been created
for emulating the output from \EB{} codes and $N$-body simulations
for both standard and extended cosmological models, including
CMBwarp \cite{jimenezFastCosmologicalParameter2004},
PICO \cite{fendtPicoParametersImpatient2007,fendtComputingHighAccuracy2007},
CosmicNet \cite{albersCosmicNetPhysicsdrivenImplementation2019,guntherCosmicNetIIEmulating2022},
CosmoPower \cite{manciniCOSMOPOWEREmulatingCosmological2022b,pirasCosmoPowerJAXHighdimensionalBayesian2023},
BACCO \cite{zennaroBACCOSimulationProject2023a},
CONNECT \cite{nygaardCONNECTNeuralNetwork2023},
Capse.jl \cite{boniciCapsejlEfficientAutodifferentiable2024},
Effort.jl \cite{boniciEffortFastDifferentiable2025a},
emulators for modified gravity simulations \cite{wintherEmulatorsNonlinearMatter2019a,maulandSesamePowerSpectrum2024a},
and many more.

When used as drop-in replacements for \EB{} codes wit high-precision settings in parameter inference calls,
emulators can speed up theoretical predictions by up to $10^6 \times$
and reduce the total MCMC runtime from days or weeks to seconds or minutes
\cite[e.g.][]{boniciCapsejlEfficientAutodifferentiable2024}.
This shifts the bottleneck of the computation from the theoretical predictions
to the comparison with the observational dataset (i.e. the likelihood function).

\Cref{chap:nonlin} effectively presents an emulation method for $N$-body simulations
that maps the expensive $N$-body simulations down to cheap \EB{} codes.
\Cref{chap:lin} presents a new interpolation method inside SymBoltz
that is enabled by the smoothness of its approximation-free output.
I also argue how I believe we could benefit from moving our dependence on approximation schemes
from the internal model-dependent layer implemented inside codes
to the model-independent layer \emph{around} the code.

\chapter{The linear part: SymBoltz}
\label{chap:lin}

Since the universe is almost homogeneous and isotropic,
it makes sense to do perturbation theory around a perfectly homogeneous and isotropic background universe.
Cosmological perturbation theory is a systematic theoretical framework for this,
and lets us study the evolution of the universe and the growth of structure.
It formulates a number of analytical equations that describe the universe,
which take the form of ordinary differential equations (ODEs)
that must be solved numerically by \EB{} codes such as CAMB and CLASS.
This is challenging because the equations are long, large and stiff;
yet the codes should be fast to be run in MCMC chains,
and simple to modify with alternative cosmological models.
To this end,
the majority of my work has been invested
in developing a new \EB{} solver named SymBoltz.

\Cref{paper:symboltz} in this thesis describes the initial implementation of SymBoltz;
including how it is made easier for users to modify the equations through a mixed symbolic-numeric interface,
handle stiffness with implicit ODE solvers instead of deriving complicated approximations of the equations,
and support new numerical methods such as automatic differentiation for obtaining accurate gradients of its output.

\Cref{paper:chebyshev} shows how SymBoltz can make use of more efficient interpolation schemes due to the smoothness of its approximation-free output.
Interpolation is very important to reduce the computational work inside these codes.

\Cref{paper:interactions} shows how a class of
modified cosmological models with non-standard interactions between baryons, cold dark matter and dark energy
are implemented in SymBoltz.
It demonstrates that less work is necessary than in CAMB and CLASS,
both due to the symbolic-numeric interface and the lack of approximation schemes.

In this chapter, I briefly introduce cosmological perturbation theory
and give an overview of the structure and history of \EB{} codes.
I then review how they have been widely modified to implement many different extended cosmological models.
This motivates the ideas behind the design of SymBoltz,
which I reiterate in this thesis from a broader perspective of cosmological research as a whole than in the paper.
I also go through some improvements that have been made since the initial implementation,
before closing with the current status of SymBoltz and an outlook to how it may be used in the future.

\section{Cosmological perturbation theory}
\label{sec:cpt}

The idea of cosmological perturbation theory is that the universe is \emph{almost} homogeneous and isotropic,
and to do perturbation theory around a perfectly homogeneous and isotropic FLRW metric $\bar{g}_{\mu\nu} = a^2(\tau) \, \eta_{\mu\nu}$,
where $\tau$ is conformal time and $\eta_{\mu\nu}$ is the Minkowski metric.
In linear perturbation theory,
every variable is expanded into an $O(\epsilon^0)$ background term and an $O(\epsilon^1)$ linear perturbation term, such as the metric and energy-momentum tensor:
\begin{equation}
g_{\mu\nu} = a^2(\tau) (\eta_{\mu\nu} + h_{\mu\nu}) ,
\qquad
T_{\mu\nu} = \bar{T}_{\mu\nu} + \delta T_{\mu\nu} ,
\qquad
(h_{\mu\nu} \ll 1, \, \delta T_{\mu\nu} \ll \bar{T}_{\mu\nu}) .
\end{equation}
Before writing down any particular theory and equations,
I summarize some general and subtle considerations that make up the theoretical framework of cosmological perturbation theory
\cite[e.g.][]{dodelsonModernCosmology2021a,baumannNotes}.

\subsection{Scalar-vector-tensor decomposition}
\label{sec:svt}

By definition, $h_{\mu\nu}$ is a symmetric $4 \times 4$ metric tensor perturbation and thus has 10 independent components.
If we are too eager and attack the Einstein equations directly with $h_{\mu\nu}$,
we will end up with a system of equations that unnecessarily couples all 10 components.
It turns out that it is a good idea to first split $h_{\mu\nu}$ into the scalar $h_{00}$, 3-vector $h_{0i}$ and symmetric 3-tensor $h_{ij}$,
and in turn make a scalar-vector-tensor (SVT) decomposition of each of them:
\begin{subequations}
\begin{align}
h_{00} &= -2 A , \\
h_{0i} &= \partial_i B + B_i, \\
h_{ij} &= 2 C \delta_{ij} + 2 \big( \partial_i \partial_j - \tfrac13 \delta_{ij} \nabla^2 \big) E + \big( \partial_i E_j + \partial_j E_i \big) + 2 E_{ij} .
\end{align}
\end{subequations}
Here $\{A,B,C,E\}$ are scalars (i.e. 4 free functions),
$\{B_i,E_i\}$ are transverse (divergence-free) vectors with $\partial^i B_i = \partial^i E_i = 0$ (i.e. $6-2=4$ free functions),
and $E_{ij}$ is a symmetric transverse-traceless tensor with $\partial^i E_{ij} = E^i_{\ i} = 0$ (i.e. $6-4=2$ free functions).
Such a decomposition is mathematically always possible.%
\footnote{The vector decomposition is also called the Helmholtz decomposition, and is familiar from electromagnetism.
The tensor decomposition is the same idea generalized to one higher rank.}
This is the final decomposition of the 10 components $h_{\mu\nu}$ into
4 scalar, 4 vector and 2 tensor functions.

The crucial point of the decomposition is that the linear perturbations of the Einstein equations for scalar, vector and tensor modes \emph{decouple}.
%This is found mathematically by working through the Einstein equations by brute force,
%and can be understood physically due to scalars, vectors and tensors transforming in different ways on the FLRW background.
The decoupling makes it easier to reason about them physically and understand what sources each mode,
and to solve them numerically as three smaller systems rather than one large system.
Without the decomposition, we would unnecessarily mix all 10 components
and solve for them in an overcomplicated way.

Here we consider only scalar perturbations, which are most important.
They drive the temperature fluctuations and E-mode polarization of the CMB
and the growth of large-scale structure.
%Vector perturbations have few sources and die out as the universe expands,
%while tensor perturbations are gravitational waves produced in the early universe and an increasingly viable observational probe.
%Scalar perturbations are associated with density fluctuations and determine the CMB temperature and E-mode polarization power spectra and the growth of large-scale structure.
%Vector perturbations have few sources and decay as the universe expands, so they are usually ignored.
%Tensor perturbations are gravitational waves, which are generated by inflation in the early universe and a promising observational probe.
%We will here consider only scalar perturbations, that are most important and the only mode currently implemented in SymBoltz.

\subsection{Gauge freedom}
\label{sec:gauge}

Another concern is that the laws of physics should not depend on our labeling of spacetime coordinates $x^\mu$.
If we make an infinitesimal coordinate transformation $x^\mu \to \tilde{x}^\mu = x^\mu + \xi^\mu(x)$,
then the metric $g_{\mu\nu} \to \tilde{g}_{\mu\nu}$ and its perturbation $h_{\mu\nu} \to \tilde{h}_{\mu\nu}$ also change
in order to preserve the spacetime interval $\diff{s}^2 = g_{\mu\nu} \, \diff{x}^\mu \diff{x}^\nu = \tilde{g}_{\mu\nu} \, \diff{\tilde{x}}^\mu \diff{\tilde{x}}^\nu$.
%Through the four degrees of freedom in $\xi^\mu(x)$ we can change $h_{\mu\nu}$.
%we have the power to arbitrarily change $h_{\mu\nu}$.
This means that 4 of 10 degrees of freedom are unphysical \enquote{gauge} degrees of freedom due to an arbitrary transformation of the coordinates,
while there are only 6 remaining truly physical degrees of freedom.

To show how this affects each of the scalar-vector-tensor modes,
one can make a similar scalar-vector decomposition of $\xi^\mu$ with $\xi^0 = T$ and $\xi^i = \partial_i L + L_i$,
where $\{T,L\}$ are scalars and $L_i$ is a transverse vector with $\partial^i L_i = 0$.
This leads to the following change in the scalar-vector-tensor parts \cite[e.g.][]{baumannNotes}:
\begin{equation}
\begin{alignedat}{3}
% TODO: how to get 3 perfectly spaced columns while preserving 1 eq number?
A &\to \tilde{A} = A - \dot{T} - \tfrac{\dot{a}}{a} T, \qquad &
B_i &\to \tilde{B}_i = B_i - \dot{L}_i, \qquad &
E_{ij} &\to \tilde{E}_{ij} = E_{ij}, \\
B &\to \tilde{B} = B + T - \dot{L}, \qquad&
E_i &\to \tilde{E}_i = E_i - L_i, \qquad& \\
C &\to \tilde{C} = C - \tfrac{\dot{a}}{a} T - \tfrac13\nabla^2 L, \qquad & & \\
E &\to \tilde{E} = E - L. \qquad & &
\end{alignedat}
\label{eq:gauge}
\end{equation}
The scalars $\{T,L\}$ influence only the scalars $\{A,B,C,E\}$ and
the vector $L_i$ (with the transverse constraint) influences only the vectors $B_i$ and $E_i$.
The tensorial part $E_{ij}$ is unchanged.
Thus, two scalar and two vector degrees of freedom are due to the gauge,
leaving 2 scalar, 2 vector and 2 tensor truly physical degrees of freedom.

From here we have two options.
One is to seek gauge-invariant variables that do not change with the gauge transformation \eqref{eq:gauge},
such as the Bardeen potentials $\Phi$ and $\Psi$ \cite{bardeenGaugeinvariantCosmologicalPerturbations1980}.
Another (and what most \EB{} codes do) is to fix one particular gauge by using our freedom in choosing $T$ and $L$.
The two most common gauges (for scalar modes only) are
\cite{maCosmologicalPerturbationTheory1995a}:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{The synchronous gauge} is defined by
$A=B=0$
(i.e. a transformation with $\{T,L\}$ such that $\dot{T} + \frac{\dot{a}}{a} T = A$ and $\dot{L}-T = B$).
\item \textbf{The (conformal) Newtonian gauge} is defined by
$B=E=0$
(i.e. a transformation with $\{T,L\}$ such that $\dot{L} - T = B$ and $L = E$).
\end{itemize}
Here we work in the conformal Newtonian gauge,
which is the only gauge currently implemented in SymBoltz.
Ultimately, the transformation \eqref{eq:gauge} can be used
to relate variables in one gauge to another
or to construct gauge-invariant variables.
Of course, observables must be built from gauge-invariant combinations of variables.

We can now write down the perturbed FLRW metric in the Newtonian gauge:
\begin{equation}
\diff{s}^2 = a^2(\tau) \left( -\big(1+2\psi(\tau, \mathbf{x})\big)\diff{\tau}^2 + \big(1-2\phi(\tau, \mathbf{x})\big) \diff{\mathbf{x}}^2 \right)
\quad (\phi,\psi \ll 1) .
\label{eq:flrw_perturbed}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Fourier space}
\label{sec:fourier}

When we do linear perturbation theory
on a homogeneous and isotropic background
every perturbation equation we encounter will come in the form
\begin{equation}
%c(\tau) \, \partial_\tau^{n_\tau} \, \partial_x^{n_x} \, \partial_y^{n_y} \, \partial_z^{n_z} \, \deltaf(\tau, \mathbf{x}) ,
\sum_i C_i(\tau) \, \partial_\tau^{a_i} \, \partial_x^{b_i} \, \partial_y^{c_i} \, \partial_z^{d_i} \, \deltaf_i(\tau, \mathbf{x}) = 0.
\label{eq:pert_real}
\end{equation}
Here $C_i(\tau)$ are time-dependent functions of the background
that cannot depend on $\mathbf{x}$ because it is homogeneous,
and $\deltaf_i(\tau, \mathbf{x})$ are perturbation variables
that appear linearly in every term and are acted upon by any number of time and space derivatives.
This is a partial differential equation (PDE) in 4 variables $(\tau, \mathbf{x})$,
and is difficult to solve in position space.

We therefore move to Fourier space $(\tau, \mathbf{k})$ by transforming every perturbation:
%(I rely on context or explicit arguments to distinguish $\deltaf(\tau, \mathbf{x})$ and $\deltaf(\tau, \mathbf{k})$):%
%\begin{subequations}
%\begin{align}
\begin{equation}
\deltaf(\tau, \mathbf{k}) = \int \diff{^3 x} \, e^{-i \mathbf{k} \cdot \mathbf{x}} \, \deltaf(\tau, \mathbf{x}), \qquad %\label{eq:r2f}
\deltaf(\tau, \mathbf{x}) = \int \frac{\diff{^3 k}}{(2\pi)^3} \, e^{+i \mathbf{k} \cdot \mathbf{x}} \, \deltaf(\tau, \mathbf{k}). %\label{eq:f2r}
\end{equation}
%\end{align}
%\end{subequations}
Transforming equation \eqref{eq:pert_real},
the corresponding equation for $\deltaf(\tau, \mathbf{k})$ becomes
%is found with the following simple prescription:
%\begin{equation}
%\pdv{f(\tau, \mathbf{x})}{\tau} \to \pdv{f(\tau, \mathbf{k})}{\tau} , \qquad
%\pdv{f(\tau, \mathbf{x})}{x_i} \to (i k_i) \cdot f(\tau, \mathbf{k}) .
%\partial_\tau \, \deltaf(\tau, \mathbf{x}) \to \partial_\tau \, \deltaf(\tau, \mathbf{k}) , \qquad
%\partial_{x_i} \, \deltaf(\tau, \mathbf{x}) \to (i k_i) \, \deltaf(\tau, \mathbf{k}) .
%\end{equation}
%In other words, equation \eqref{eq:pert_real} in Fourier space is
\begin{equation}
\sum_i C_i(\tau) \, (i k_x)^{b_i} (i k_y)^{c_i} (i k_z)^{d_i} \, \partial_\tau^{a_i} \, \deltaf_i(\tau, \mathbf{k}) = 0.
\label{eq:pert_fourier}
\end{equation}
This is a set of ordinary differential equations (ODEs) in time $\tau$ for each wavenumber $\mathbf{k}$,
where different $\mathbf{k}$ enters \emph{independently}.
This decoupling happens due to linearity (one $\deltaf$ per term)
and homogeneity ($C_i(\tau)$ independent of $\mathbf{x}$).
Then only one $\mathbf{k}$-integral is inserted per term,
so all terms of equation \eqref{eq:pert_real} combine under a single $\mathbf{k}$-integral
whose integrand must vanish separately per $\mathbf{k}$.
There is never any convolution between different $\mathbf{k}$.
It is far easier to solve many small ODEs \eqref{eq:pert_fourier} in Fourier space
than one big four-dimensional PDE \eqref{eq:pert_real} in position space.

\section{\EB{} equations}

Having laid the groundwork in \cref{sec:cpt},
we move on to the fundamental \EB{} equations of cosmology.
These coupled equations describe the interplay between gravity (through the Einstein equation)
and the \enquote{matter} ingredients that make up the universe (through the Boltzmann equation).

Here I summarize how scalar modes (see \cref{sec:svt})
in the conformal Newtonian gauge \eqref{eq:flrw_perturbed} (see \cref{sec:gauge})
evolve up to linear order in Fourier space (see \cref{sec:fourier}).
I highlight the general structure of the equations in the seminal work of \cite{maCosmologicalPerturbationTheory1995a},
starting with general considerations and then simplifying the equations in special cases.

\subsection{Einstein equations}
\label{sec:einstein}

Gravity in \GR{} is described by the Einstein equations
\begin{equation}
G_{\mu\nu} = 8\pi T_{\mu\nu} .
\label{eq:einstein}
\end{equation}
On the left is the Einstein tensor $G_{\mu\nu} = R_{\mu\nu} - \frac12 R g_{\mu\nu}$,
which is built from the Ricci scalar $R = R^\mu_\mu$,
Ricci tensor $R_{\mu\nu} = R^{\rho}_{\mu\rho\nu}$,
Riemann tensor $R^{\rho}_{\sigma\mu\nu}$,
Christoffel symbols $\Gamma^{\alpha}_{\beta\gamma}$,
and ultimately the spacetime metric $g_{\mu\nu}$ \cite[e.g.][]{dodelsonModernCosmology2021a}.
On the right is the energy-momentum tensor $T_{\mu\nu}$ that describes the \enquote{matter} content of the universe.
Here we absorb the cosmological constant $\Lambda$ into $T_{\mu\nu}$ (see \cref{sec:LCDM}).

Now insert the perturbed metric \eqref{eq:flrw_perturbed}
and compute up to 1st order in perturbation theory.
In the background (to 0th order),
we get the Friedmann equations \eqref{eq:fried} for the scale factor $a(\tau)$
with respect to conformal time $\tau$:%
\begin{subequations}
\begin{align}
\calH^2 &= \left( \frac{\dot{a}}{a} \right)^2 = \frac{8\pi}{3} \, a^2 \rho , \label{eq:bgfried1} \\
\dot{\calH} &= -\frac{4\pi}{3} \, a^2 (\rho+3P) \label{eq:bgfried2}.
\end{align}
\end{subequations}
In the perturbations (to 1st order),
we get four (redundant) equations for the metric perturbations $\phi$ and $\psi$:
\begin{subequations}
\label{eq:pertein}
\begin{align}
k^2 \phi + 3\calH(\dot{\phi} + \calH \psi) &= -4\pi a^2 \delta\rho, \label{eq:pertein1} \\
k^2 (\dot\phi + \calH \psi) &= 4\pi a^2 (\rho+P)\theta, \label{eq:pertein2} \\
\ddot{\phi} + \calH (\dot{\psi}+2\dot{\phi}) + (2\dot{\calH}+\calH^2) \psi + \tfrac13 k^2 (\phi-\psi) &= 4\pi a^2 \delta P , \label{eq:pertein3} \\
k^2 (\phi-\psi) &= 12 \pi a^2 (\rho+P)\sigma. \label{eq:pertein4}
\end{align}
\end{subequations}
On the right we have introduced several variables for the energy-momentum perturbations $\delta T^\mu{}_\nu$
(see \cite{maCosmologicalPerturbationTheory1995a} for precise definitions;
the variables are computed in \cref{sec:boltzmann}):%
\footnote{Note that $T_{\mu\nu}$ is not a perfect fluid at this general level!
This is only true for the contribution from some species in \cref{sec:fluid}.}
\begin{itemize}
\item Equation \eqref{eq:pertein1} describes how energy density perturbations $\delta\rho \propto \delta T^0_0$ (i.e. presence of energy) alter the 1st Friedmann equation locally.
\item Equation \eqref{eq:pertein2} describes how the momentum density divergence $(\rho+P)\theta \propto i k^j \delta T^0_j$ (i.e. flux of energy) determines the metric in a redundant way.
\item Equation \eqref{eq:pertein3} describes how pressure perturbations $\delta P$ (from the trace part of $\delta T^i_j$) alter the Friedmann acceleration equation locally.
\item Equation \eqref{eq:pertein4} describes how anisotropic stresses $(\rho+P)\sigma$ (from the traceless part of $\delta T^i_j$) induce a \enquote{slip} $\phi \neq \psi$ between the metric potentials.
\end{itemize}

From purely geometrical considerations,
one can derive the Bianchi identity $\nabla_\mu G^{\mu\nu} = 0$ on the left side of the Einstein equations \eqref{eq:einstein}.
On the right side, it then follows immediately that the total $T_{\mu\nu}$ obeys the conservation law $\nabla_\mu T^{\mu\nu} = 0$.
This leads to the background continuity equation for the total $\rho$
in terms of the total equation of state $w = P/\rho$:
\begin{equation}
\dot{\rho} = -3\calH(1+w) \rho.
\label{eq:bg_cont}
\end{equation}
We also get the perturbed continuity and Euler equations for $\delta=\delta\rho/\rho$ and $\theta$:
\begin{subequations}
\label{eq:pert_fluid}
\begin{align}
\dot{\delta} &= -(1+w)(\theta-3\dot{\phi}) - 3\calH \left( \frac{\delta P}{\delta \rho} - w \right) \delta, \label{eq:pert_cont} \\
\dot{\theta} &= -(1-3w)\calH\theta - \frac{\dot{w}}{1+w}\theta + \frac{\delta P/\delta \rho}{1+w}k^2\delta - k^2\sigma + k^2 \psi. \label{eq:pert_euler}
\end{align}
\end{subequations}
This describes the evolution of the total energy-momentum contributions,
but is not a closed system of equations because the Einstein equations do not say anything about $w$, $\delta P/\delta\rho$ and $\sigma$.
To close the system,
we will describe individual contributions from different species with the Boltzmann equation.

\subsection{Boltzmann equation}
\label{sec:boltzmann}

The universe contains a lot of different \enquote{stuff}.
We therefore describe the total energy-momentum $T_{\mu\nu} = \sum_s T_{\mu\nu}^s$ of matter
on the right sides of the Einstein equations \eqref{eq:pertein}
as a sum of contributions from several species $s$:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:totalT}
\begin{aligned}
\rho &= \sum_s \rho_s, \qquad &
\delta\rho &= \sum_s \delta\rho_s, \qquad &
(\rho+P)\theta &= \sum_s (\rho_s+P_s)\theta_s, \\
P &= \sum_s P_s, \qquad &
\delta P &= \sum_s \delta P_s, \qquad &
(\rho+P)\sigma &= \sum_s (\rho_s+P_s)\sigma_s .
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
Note that $\delta_s$, $\theta_s$ and $\sigma_s$ are \emph{not} additive.
As far as gravity is concerned,
these are the only quantities we need to compute for each species.

We use statistical mechanics to describe the distribution function $f(\tau, \mathbf{x}, \mathbf{q})$ of each species in phase space of (comoving) positions $\mathbf{x}$ and momenta $\mathbf{q}$,
and track time $\tau$ explicitly as the first argument.%
\footnote{There are many subtleties in the full theoretical derivation here,
such as the distinction between the 4-momentum $P^\mu$ which enters the geodesic equation,
the conjugate variables $x^i$ and $P_i$ in Hamiltonian mechanics in curved spacetime,
and the comoving momentum $\mathbf{q} = a \mathbf{p}$.
See \cite[section 5.1]{maCosmologicalPerturbationTheory1995a} for more details.
The end result is that we may write $f(\tau, \mathbf{x}, \mathbf{q})$.
}
To do perturbation theory,
we split it into a homogeneous and isotropic background term $f_0(\tau, q)$ and perturbation:
\begin{equation}
f(\tau, \mathbf{x}, \mathbf{q}) = f_0(\tau, q) \, \big(1 + \Psi(\tau, \mathbf{x}, \mathbf{q})\big)
\qquad (\Psi \ll 1) .
\label{eq:distribution}
\end{equation}
The distribution function in turn determines the energy-momentum
\begin{equation}
T^0{}_0 = -a^{-4} \int \diff^3{q} \, \epsilon \, f , \ \ \quad
T^0{}_i = a^{-4} \int \diff^3{q} \, q_i \, f , \ \ \quad
T^i{}_j = a^{-4} \int \diff^3{q} \, \frac{q_i q_j}{\epsilon} \, f ,
\label{eq:Tgeneral}
\end{equation}
with the comoving energy
\begin{equation}
\epsilon(\tau, q) = \sqrt{q^2 + a(\tau)^2 m^2} .
\label{eq:comoving_energy}
\end{equation}

The Boltzmann equation describes the evolution of the distribution in phase space
for the total time derivative along a trajectory $(\tau, \mathbf{x}(\tau), \mathbf{q}(\tau))$:
\begin{equation}
\odv{f}{\tau} = \pdv{f}{\tau} + \pdv{f}{x_i} \odv{x_i}{\tau} + \pdv{f}{q_i} \odv{q_i}{\tau} = C[f] .
\label{eq:boltzmann}
\end{equation}
The collision term $C[f]$ says how the density of particles at each point in phase space
is modified due to interactions (\enquote{collisions}) with other species (or self-interactions).
The argument means that it is a functional of the distribution functions of all the interacting species.
It vanishes for non-interacting species (\enquote{collisionless}).

In the background,
the geodesic equation can be used to show that the comoving momentum $q$ is constant along trajectories.
The Boltzmann equation is then just
\begin{equation}
\pdv{f_0}{\tau} = C_0[f_0].
\label{eq:boltzmann_bg}
\end{equation}
Notably, it means that we can write $f_0(\tau, q) = f_0(q)$ for non-interacting species.
From the energy-momentum tensor \eqref{eq:Tgeneral}, the background density and pressure are:
\begin{equation}
\rho = 4\pi a^{-4} \int_0^\infty \diff{q}\,q^2 f_0 \, \epsilon , \qquad
P = \frac{4\pi}{3} a^{-4} \int_0^\infty \diff{q}\,q^2 f_0 \, \frac{q^2}{\epsilon} .
\label{eq:energy_momentum_integrals_bg}
\end{equation}

For the perturbation $\Psi$,
expressing $\odv{q}/{\tau}$ with the geodesic equation,
collecting terms to first order and going to Fourier space gives:
\begin{equation}
\pdv{\Psi}{\tau} + i \, \frac{\mathbf{k} \cdot \mathbf{q}}{\epsilon} \Psi + \odv{\log f_0}{\log q} \left(\dot{\phi} - i \, \mathbf{k} \cdot \mathbf{q} \, \frac{\epsilon}{q^2} \psi \right) = \frac{\delta C[f]}{f_0}.
\label{eq:boltzmann_linear}
\end{equation}
This perturbed Boltzmann equation is solved in different ways for different species.

\subsubsection{Hierarchy with multipoles and momenta: the general treatment}

The perturbed Boltzmann equation \eqref{eq:boltzmann_linear}
depends on $\mathbf{k}$ and $\mathbf{q}$ only through their magnitudes $k$ and $q$
and relative direction $\mu = \hat{\mathbf{k}} \cdot \hat{\mathbf{q}} \in [-1, 1]$.
The same is true for $\delta\rho$, $\delta P$, $\theta$ and $\sigma$,
which are the energy-momentum integrals \eqref{eq:Tgeneral} weighted by zero, one or two wavenumber components $k^i$,
and thus in the form $\int \diff^3{q} = 2\pi \int \diff{q} \, q^2 \int \diff{\mu}$.

Due to this azimuthal symmetry,
it is natural to redefine $\Psi(\tau, \mathbf{k}, \mathbf{q}) \to \Psi(\tau, k, q, \mu)$
and then expand it in Legendre polynomials $P_l(\mu)$:
\begin{equation}
\Psi(\tau, k, q, \mu) = \sum_{l=0}^\infty (-i)^l (2l+1) \Psi_l(\tau, k, q) P_l(\mu) .
\label{eq:decompose_legendre}
\end{equation}

The variables that source gravity
are found from the energy-momentum \eqref{eq:Tgeneral}
in terms of only the monopole $\Psi_0$, dipole $\Psi_1$ and quadrupole $\Psi_2$:
\begin{subequations}
\begin{align}
\delta\rho &= 4\pi a^{-4} \int_0^\infty \diff{q}\,q^2 f_0 \, \epsilon \, \Psi_0, &
(\rho + P)\theta &= 4\pi k a^{-4} \int_0^\infty \diff{q}\,q^2 f_0 \, q \, \Psi_1, \\
\delta P &= \frac{4\pi}{3} a^{-4} \int_0^\infty \diff{q}\,q^2 f_0 \, \frac{q^2}{\epsilon} \, \Psi_0, &
(\rho + P)\sigma &= \frac{8\pi}{3} a^{-4} \int_0^\infty \diff{q}\,q^2 f_0 \, \frac{q^2}{\epsilon} \, \Psi_2 .
\end{align}%
\label{eq:energy_momentum_integrals_pert}%
\end{subequations}

With the multipole expansion, the linearized Boltzmann equation \eqref{eq:boltzmann_linear}
becomes a \enquote{hierarchy} for the evolution of each multipole $\Psi_l(\tau, k, q)$.
Ignoring interactions and using the identity $\mu P_l(\mu) = (l P_{l-1}(\mu) + (l+1) P_{l+1}(\mu)) / (2l+1)$,
we get:
\begin{subequations}
\label{eq:hierarchy_ql}
\begin{align}
\dot{\Psi}_0 &= -k \frac{q}{\epsilon} \Psi_1 - \dot{\phi}\,\odv{\log f_0}{\log q} , \\
\dot{\Psi}_1 &= \frac{k}{3} \frac{q}{\epsilon} \big(\Psi_0 - 2\Psi_2\big) - \frac{k}{3} \frac{\epsilon}{q} \psi \odv{\log f_0}{\log q} , \\
\dot{\Psi}_l &= \frac{k}{2l+1} \frac{q}{\epsilon}\big(l \Psi_{l-1} - (l+1) \Psi_{l+1}\big) \qquad\quad (l \geq 2) .
\end{align}
\end{subequations}
Note that the multipole expansion couples $l$ to just $l \pm 1$,
and that there is one differential equation for every $q$.
Although the Einstein equations only need $l \leq 2$,
the time evolution still couples all $l \geq 0$.

This treatment is valid for arbitrary species, $f_0$ and $m$ (here in the non-interacting case).
It is necessary for massive neutrinos, which transition from ultra-relativistic to non-relativistic limits
as the expansion slows their momenta.
Other species fall into limiting regimes where it is possible to integrate out $q$ or cut the multipole evolution at a finite $l$.

\subsubsection{Hierarchy with multipoles: the relativistic treatment}

For species that are relativistic with $m=0$ (or $q/a \gg m$),
we can analytically integrate out the $q$-dependence of $\Psi$
to obtain a simpler and smaller set of equations.

Define the momentum-averaged distribution function (multipoles)
\begin{equation}
F(\tau, k, \mu) = \frac{\int_0^\infty \diff{q} \, q^2 f_0 \, q \, \Psi(\tau, k, q, \mu)}{\int_0^\infty \diff{q} \, q^2 f_0 \, q} , \quad
F_l(\tau, k) = \frac{\int_0^\infty \diff{q} \, q^2 f_0 \, q \, \Psi_l(\tau, k, q)}{\int_0^\infty \diff{q} \, q^2 f_0 \, q} .
\label{eq:Fl}
\end{equation}
Then integrate the hierarchy \eqref{eq:hierarchy_ql} with $\epsilon=q$ over $4\pi \int \diff{q} \, q^2 f_0 \, q$
and use that $\int \diff{q} \, q^3 f_0 \odv{\log f_0}/{\log q} = \int \diff{q} \, q^4 \odv{f_0}/{q} = -4 \int \diff{q} \, q^3 f_0$ by parts.
Ultimately:
\begin{subequations}
\begin{align}
\dot{F}_0 &= -k F_1 + 4 \dot{\phi} , \\
\dot{F}_1 &= \frac{k}{3} \big(F_0 - 2 F_2 + 4\psi\big) , \\
\dot{F}_l &= \frac{k}{2l+1} \big(l F_{l-1} - (l+1) F_{l+1}\big) , \quad (l \geq 2) .
\end{align}%
\label{eq:hierarchy_l}%
\end{subequations}
The reduced hierarchy for $F_l(\tau, k)$ is still infinite over $l$,
but $q$ has been fully integrated out.
We also get $w = P/\rho = \delta P/\delta\rho = 1/3$, $\delta = F_0$, $\theta = 3 k F_1 / 4$ and $\sigma = F_2 / 2$.

\subsubsection{Fluid treatment}
\label{sec:fluid}

The simplest way to model a species is to describe it as a \enquote{fluid}.
The goal is to integrate out $q$ for any $m$,
and finally truncate the hierarchy at low $l$
by closing the system with additional equations of state.

Let us start with the background Boltzmann equation \eqref{eq:boltzmann_bg}.
Multiply it by $\epsilon$
and use the product rule $\dot{f}_0 \, \epsilon = \dot{(f_0 \, \epsilon)} - f_0 \, \dot{\epsilon}$.
Then use that the time derivative of the comoving energy \eqref{eq:comoving_energy}
is $\dot{\epsilon} = \calH a^2 m^2 / \epsilon = \calH(\epsilon - q^2/\epsilon)$.
Finally integrate over $4\pi \int \diff{q} \, q^2$,
and reverse-engineer $\rho$ and $P$ from the integrals \eqref{eq:energy_momentum_integrals_bg}.
Ignoring interactions, this gives the background continuity equation:
\TODO{$Q$?}
\begin{equation}
\dot{\rho} = -3 \calH (\rho + P) = -3 \calH (1+w) \rho .
\label{eq:fluid_continuity_bg}
\end{equation}
It has the analytical solution $\rho \propto a^{-3(1+w)}$
if $w=P/\rho$ is constant in time.

Now follow a similar strategy with the general perturbed $(q,l)$-hierarchy \eqref{eq:hierarchy_ql}.
Integrate the $l=0$ equation over $\int \diff{q}\,q^2 f_0 \, \epsilon$
and use the product rule and $\dot{\epsilon}$ from above again.
Integrate the term with $\odv{\log f_0}/{\log q}$ by parts in $q$,
and collect the right sides of the integrals \eqref{eq:energy_momentum_integrals_pert}.
This gives the perturbed continuity equation:
\TODO{$\delta Q$ etc?}
\begin{subequations}
\label{eq:fluid}
\begin{equation}
\dot{\delta} = -(1+w)(\theta-3\dot{\phi}) - 3\calH \left( \frac{\delta P}{\delta \rho} - w \right) \delta .
\label{eq:fluid_continuity}
\end{equation}
Do the same with the $l=1$ equation,
but integrate over $\int \diff{q}\,q^2 f_0 q$ instead.
This gives the perturbed Euler equation:
\begin{equation}
\dot{\theta} = -(1-3w)\calH\theta - \frac{\dot{w}}{1+w}\theta + \frac{\delta P/\delta \rho}{1+w}k^2\delta - k^2\sigma + k^2 \psi .
\label{eq:fluid_euler}
\end{equation}
The same can be done for $l \geq 2$.
Integrating the $l=2$ equation over $\int \diff{q} \, q^2 f_0 \, q^2 / \epsilon$
gives a differential equation for the anisotropic stress $\sigma$
\cite[e.g.][]{nascimentoGeneralizedBoltzmannHierarchy2021,lesgourguesCosmicLinearAnisotropy2011f}:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\dot\sigma &= -\calH\,(2-3w)\,\sigma - \frac{\dot w}{1+w}\,\sigma \\
&+ \frac{8\pi\,a^{-4}}{\rho+ P}\left[
\frac{\calH}{3}\int\!\diff{q}\,q^2 f_0\,\frac{q^4}{\epsilon^3}\,\Psi_2
+ \frac{k}{15}\int\!\diff{q}\,q^2 f_0\,\frac{q^3}{\epsilon^2}\big(2\Psi_1 - 3\Psi_3\big)
\right] .
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:fluid_shear}
\end{equation}
\end{subequations}
This is consistent with the conservation \eqref{eq:pert_fluid} of the Einstein equations but for \emph{individual} species,
and also allows us to understand how the fluid variables arise from the distribution function.
We also get equations for $\sigma$ and all other $l \geq 2$,
which gravity cannot give us.
We could also have obtained interaction terms in terms of similar integrals over the collision term,
had we not ignored them.

A \enquote{fluid} refers to a species where the $q$-dependence is integrated out in this way
\emph{and} the equations are closed with additional equations of state $w$,
sound speed $\delta P/\delta \rho$ and shear $\sigma$.
For example, for standard species with fluid descriptions,
$w$ is a constant or simple function of time,
$\delta P = w$ and $\sigma = 0$
(see \cref{sec:eb_full}).
This effectively truncates the hierarchy at $l \leq 1$ or $l \leq 2$ (if $\sigma$ is evolved).

\subsection{Fully coupled \EB{} system}
\label{sec:eb_full}

The fundamentals of everything in the \EB{} system is given by \cref{sec:einstein,sec:boltzmann}.
However, there are several nuances in the treatment of each species that would be far too lengthy to include in this thesis,
such as the detailed treatment of interactions.
Ultimately, the \LCDM{} results in a large system of ODEs and algebraic relations that must be evolved in time,
as I highlight here.

\begin{subequations}
\label{eq:eb}

\subsubsection{Gravity (\texorpdfstring{$G$}{G})}
Gravity is described by the Einstein equations of General Relativity (as in \cref{sec:einstein}).
The two background Friedmann equations \eqref{eq:fried} are redundant equations for $a$;
it is most common to use the square root of the 1st equation \eqref{eq:fried1} as a first order ODE.
Similarly, the four perturbed equations \eqref{eq:pertein} are redunant for $\phi$ and $\psi$;
it is most common to integrate the first order ODE \eqref{eq:pertein1} for $\dot{\phi}$
and determine $\psi$ algebraically from the constraint \eqref{eq:pertein4}.
These equations are sourced by the total $\rho$, $P$, $\delta\rho$ and $(\rho+P)\sigma$.
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:eb_gravity}
\begin{aligned}
\dot{a}(\tau) &= a^2 \sqrt{\tfrac{8\pi}{3} \rho} , \\
\rho(\tau) &= \rho_b + \rho_c + \rho_\gamma + \rho_\nu + \rho_h + \rho_\Lambda , \\
P(\tau) &= P_\gamma + P_\nu + P_h + P_\Lambda , \\
\dot{\phi}(\tau, k) &= -\calH \psi - \frac{k^2}{3\calH}\phi - \frac{4\pi a^2}{3\calH}\delta\rho , \\
\psi(\tau, k) &= \phi - \frac{12\pi a^2}{k^2} (\rho+P)\sigma , \\
\delta\rho(\tau, k) &= \delta\rho_b + \delta\rho_c + \delta\rho_\gamma + \delta\rho_\nu + \delta\rho_h , \\
\sigma(\tau, k) &= \frac{(\rho_\gamma+P_\gamma)\sigma_\gamma + (\rho_\nu+P_\nu)\sigma_\nu + (\rho_h+P_h)\sigma_h}{\rho+P} .
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{Cold dark matter (\texorpdfstring{$c$}{c})}

Cold dark matter is modeled as a collisionless and pressureless perfect (non-viscous) fluid \eqref{eq:fluid} with $P = \delta P = \sigma = 0$:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:eb_cold_dark_matter}
\begin{aligned}
\rho_c(\tau) &= \rho_c(\tau_0) \, a^{-3}, \\
\dot{\delta}_c(\tau, k) &= -\theta_c + 3\dot{\phi} , \\
\dot{\theta}_c(\tau, k) &= -\calH \theta_c + k^2 \psi , \\
\delta\rho_c(\tau, k) &= \rho_c \, \delta_c .
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{Baryons (\texorpdfstring{$b$}{b})}

Baryons are modeled as a fluid (as in \cref{sec:fluid}) in the non-relativistic limit $q/a \ll m$
with small $P$, $\delta P$ and $\sigma$.
It is usually done with $w=0$ in the background and $\sigma=0$ and $c_s^2 = \delta P/\delta \rho = 0$ in the perturbed continuity equation \eqref{eq:fluid_continuity},
but $k^2 c_s^2 \neq 0$ in the Euler equation \eqref{eq:fluid_euler}.
In addition, baryons interact with photons through Thomson scattering with rate $\dot\kappa$,
which requires evolution of the free electron density $x_e$, baryon temperature $T_b$ and optical depth $\kappa$ from recombination and reionization (see \cref{sec:modify_recombination}),
and momentum exchange in the perturbations:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:eb_baryons}
\begin{aligned}
\rho_b(\tau) &= \rho_b(\tau_0) \, a^{-3} , \\
\dot{T}_b(\tau) &= -2\calH T_b + \frac{8 \sigma_T a \rho_\gamma}{3 m_e} \frac{x_e}{1+f_\mathrm{He}+x_e} (T_\gamma - T_b) , \\
\dot{x}_e(\tau) &= \dot{x}_e(a, H, x_e, T_b, T_\gamma, \ldots) , \\
\dot{\kappa}(\tau) &= a\, x_e\, n_H\, \sigma_T , \\
c_s^2(\tau) &= \frac{T_b}{\mu m_H} \left( 1 - \frac{\dot{T}_b}{3 \calH T_b} \right) , \\
\dot{\delta}_b(\tau, k) &= -\theta_b + 3\dot{\phi} , \\
\dot{\theta}_b(\tau, k) &= -\calH \theta_b + c_s^2 k^2 \delta_b + k^2 \psi + \frac{4\rho_\gamma}{3\rho_b} \dot\kappa (\theta_\gamma - \theta_b) , \\
\delta\rho_b(\tau, k) &= \rho_b \, \delta_b .
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
Here $\dot{x}_e$ must be read as a placeholder for all recombination and reionization equations,
which would significantly complicate this list if they were included.

\subsubsection{Photons (\texorpdfstring{$\gamma$}{gamma})}

Photons are massless and therefore described by a relativistic $l$-hierarchy \eqref{eq:hierarchy_l}.
However, taking their polarization into account results in the evolution of two such hierarchies $F_l$ and $G_l$ \cite[section 5.5][]{maCosmologicalPerturbationTheory1995a},
and their interactions with baryons adds collision terms proportional to $\dot\kappa$:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:eb_photons}
\begin{aligned}
\rho_\gamma(\tau) &= \rho_\gamma(\tau_0) \, a^{-4}, \\
P(\tau) &= \tfrac13 \, \rho_\gamma(\tau), \\
\dot{F}_0(\tau, k) &= -k \, F_1 + 4\dot{\phi} , \\
\dot{F}_1(\tau, k) &= \frac{k}{3}\,\big(F_0 - 2 F_2\big) + \frac{4}{3}\,k\psi + \frac43 \, \frac{\dot\kappa}{k} \left( \theta_b - \theta_\gamma \right) , \\
\dot{F}_l(\tau, k) &= \frac{k}{2l+1}\,\big(l F_{l-1} - (l+1) F_{l+1}\big) - \dot\kappa \, \big(F_l - \tfrac{1}{10}\delta_{l2}\Pi\big) , \\
\dot{G}_0(\tau, k) &= -k \, G_1 - \dot\kappa \, \big(G_0 - \tfrac{1}{2}\Pi\big) , \\
\dot{G}_1(\tau, k) &= \frac{k}{3}\,\big(G_0 - 2 G_2\big) - \dot\kappa \, G_1 , \\
\dot{G}_l(\tau, k) &= \frac{k}{2l+1}\,\big(l \, G_{l-1} - (l+1) G_{l+1}\big) - \dot\kappa \, \big(G_l - \tfrac{1}{10}\, \delta_{l2}\,\Pi \big) , \\
\dot{F}_{\lmax}(\tau, k) &= k \,F_{\lmax-1} - \frac{\lmax+1}{\tau} \, F_{\lmax} - \dot\kappa \, F_{\lmax} , \\
\dot{G}_{\lmax}(\tau, k) &= k \, G_{\lmax-1} - \frac{\lmax+1}{\tau} \, G_{\lmax} - \dot\kappa \, G_{\lmax} , \\
\Pi(\tau, k) &= F_2 + G_0 + G_2 , \\
\delta\rho_\gamma(\tau, k) &= \rho_\gamma \, F_0 , \\
\theta_\gamma(\tau, k) &= \tfrac34 k F_1, \\
\sigma_\gamma(\tau, k) &= \tfrac{1}{2} F_2 .
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
The $F_l$ and $G_l$ hierarchies are cut off at a finite $l = \lmax$
by assuming the analytical solutions $F_l \propto j_l(k\tau)$ and $G_l \propto j_l(k\tau)$
that are approximately valid for free-streaming photons at sufficiently high $l$ \cite[section 5.3]{maCosmologicalPerturbationTheory1995a}.
Due to the identity $j_l'(x) = (l\,j_{l-1}(x) - (l+1)\,j_{l+1}(x)) \, / \, (2l+1)$,
the spherical Bessel functions $j_l(x)$ solve the infinite hierarchy when $\dot\kappa = 0$.
To close the system we also eliminate $j_{l+1}(x)$
with another identity $j_{l+1}(x) = (2l+1) j_l(x) / x - j_{l-1}(x)$.

\subsubsection{Massive neutrinos (\texorpdfstring{$h$}{h})}

Massive neutrinos are parameterized by their nonzero mass $m$ and modeled as a full $(q,l)$-hierarchy \eqref{eq:hierarchy_ql}
with the Fermi-Dirac distribution function $f_0(q) \propto 1 / (\exp(q/T_{h0})+1)$.%
\footnote{
The Fermi-Dirac distribution is really $f_0(\tau, q) = 1 / (\exp(E(\tau,q)/T(\tau))+1)$,
but neutrinos decoupled from the primordial plasma while they were relativistic with $E/T_h \approx q/T_{h0}$.
From this moment, $f_0(q)$ is frozen in time by the background Boltzmann equation \eqref{eq:boltzmann_bg}.
}
Since we cannot integrate out $q$,
we must effectively evolve $\Psi_l(\tau, k, q)$ inside the momentum integrals \eqref{eq:energy_momentum_integrals_pert} in time.
To do this in practice, the idea is to assume a numerical quadrature scheme
$I[g(q)] = \int \diff{q}\,q^2 f_0(q) \, g(q) \approx \sum_{i=1}^{N_q} W_i \, g(q_i)$
to reduce the problem to the evolution of a finite number of $q_i$ points and to evaluate all integrals by the discrete sum with weights $W_i$:%
\footnote{Note that we define the weights to absorb $q^2 f_0(q)$, since this factor appears in all integrals.}
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:eb_massive_neutrinos}
\begin{aligned}
\rho_h(\tau) &= 4\pi a^{-4} \sum_i W_i\, \epsilon_i , \\
P_h(\tau) &= \frac{4\pi}{3} a^{-4} \sum_i W_i \frac{q_i^2}{\epsilon_i} , \\
\dot{\Psi}_{0,i}(\tau, k) &= -k \frac{q_i}{\epsilon_i} \Psi_{1,i} - \dot{\phi} \left(\odv{\log f_0}{\log q}\right)_i , \\
\dot{\Psi}_{1,i}(\tau, k) &= \frac{k}{3} \frac{q_i}{\epsilon_i} (\Psi_{0,i} - 2\Psi_{2,i}) - \frac{k}{3} \frac{\epsilon_i}{q_i} \psi \left(\odv{\log f_0}{\log q}\right)_i , \\
\dot{\Psi}_{l,i}(\tau, k) &= \frac{k}{2l+1} \frac{q_i}{\epsilon_i} \big(l \Psi_{l-1,i} - (l+1) \Psi_{l+1,i}\big) , \\
\dot{\Psi}_{\lmax,i}(\tau, k) &= k \frac{q_i}{\epsilon_i} \Psi_{\lmax-1,i} - \frac{\lmax+1}{\tau} \Psi_{\lmax,i} , \\
\delta\rho_h(\tau, k) &= 4\pi a^{-4} \sum_i W_i\, \epsilon_i \Psi_{0,i} , \\
\sigma_h(\tau, k) &= \frac{1}{\rho_h+P_h} \frac{8\pi}{3} a^{-4} \sum_i W_i \frac{q_i^2}{\epsilon_i} \Psi_{2,i} .
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
The $l$-hierarchy cutoff at $\lmax$ is done in the same way as for the photons.
In principle, we should evolve one such hierarchy for each of the $N_m = 3$ neutrino mass eigenstates.
Thus, massive neutrinos contribute $N_m \times (\lmax+1) \times N_q$ perturbation ODEs,
which significantly increases the size of the ODE system compared to $l$-only hierarchies!
This can be simplified by evolving a single approximate degenerate hierarchy with $m = \sum m_\nu / 3$ instead,
or by approximating some of them as massless neutrinos.

\subsubsection{Massless neutrinos (\texorpdfstring{$\nu$}{nu})}

Since the lightest neutrino mass eigenstates can remain relativistic until the late universe,
we may approximate some or all mass eigenstates as relativistic throughout \cite{lesgourguesMassiveNeutrinosCosmology2006}.
%\cite[e.g.][section 2.1]{planck18parameters}.
Such \enquote{massless neutrinos} are modeled as a relativistic $l$-hierarchy \eqref{eq:hierarchy_l} without interactions,
and effectively become a simpler version of the photon equations
(with $F_l \to N_l$):
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:eb_massless_neutrinos}
\begin{aligned}
\rho_\nu(\tau) &= \rho_\nu(\tau_0) \, a^{-4}, \\
\dot{N}_0(\tau, k) &= -k N_1 + 4\dot{\phi} , \\
\dot{N}_1(\tau, k) &= \frac{k}{3}(N_0 - 2N_2) + \frac{4k}{3}\psi , \\
\dot{N}_l(\tau, k) &= \frac{k}{2l+1}\big(l N_{l-1} - (l+1) N_{l+1}\big) , \\
\dot{N}_{\lmax}(\tau, k) &= k N_{\lmax-1} - \frac{\lmax+1}{\tau} N_{\lmax} , \\
\delta\rho_\nu(\tau, k) &= \rho_\nu N_0 , \\
\sigma_\nu(\tau, k) &= \tfrac{1}{2} N_2 .
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
Massless neutrinos have simpler equations than massive ones and reduce the size of the perturbation ODEs.

\subsubsection{Cosmological constant (\texorpdfstring{$\Lambda$}{Lambda})}

The cosmological constant $\Lambda$ is really an additional term $\Lambda g_{\mu\nu}$ on the left side of the Einstein equations \eqref{eq:einstein}.
We can effectively treat it as a fluid \eqref{eq:fluid} with $w=-1$ in the background and no perturbations:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:cc}
\begin{aligned}
\rho_\Lambda(\tau) &= \rho_\Lambda(\tau_0) , \\
P_\Lambda(\tau) &= -\rho_\Lambda .
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}

\end{subequations}

\subsubsection{Initial conditions}

We start integration at small $\tau$ in the early universe,
where we can initialize $a(\tau)$ from the radiation-dominated Friedmann equation $\calH \approx 1/\tau$.

For the perturbations we ensure that $k\tau \ll 1$,
so each $k$-mode is still \enquote{outside the horizon}
and there is not yet any causal connection at the corresponding distance scale $1/k$.
One may then derive initial conditions of the perturbation equations
for \enquote{adiabatic} or \enquote{isocurvature} perturbations
\cite[e.g.][section 7]{maCosmologicalPerturbationTheory1995a}.

Note that the perturbation equations are linear,
so their initial conditions and hence their solution are only determined up to a constant rescaling.
The true power spectrum $P_{XY}(k) = P_0(k) \, X(k) \, Y(k)$ of two perturbation variables
is found by multiplication with the primordial power spectrum predicted by inflation:
\begin{equation}
P_0(k) = \frac{2\pi^2}{k^3} \, A_s \, \left( \frac{k}{k_\mathrm{pivot}} \right)^{n_s-1} ,
\end{equation}
parameterized by the amplitude $A_s$ and tilt $n_s$.
Thus, the perturbations in the \EB{} system are best interpreted as transfer functions
from the early to the late universe.

Our focus here is on the high-level \EB{} equations,
so we do not go in great detail on initial conditions here.

\section{\EB{} codes}
\label{sec:eb_codes}

The core task of an \EB{} code is to solve the entire \EB{} system of ODEs \eqref{eq:eb}.
Afterwards, a range of post-processing steps may or may not be performed,
in order to compute observables such as distances, matter power spectra and CMB power spectra.
The order of computational operations are
\cite[e.g.][]{callinHowCalculateCMB2006b,lesgourguesCosmicLinearAnisotropy2011d,wintherscoming}:
\TODO{fix Win citation}
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Background ODEs} are integrated in $\tau$ for variables $u(\tau)$ that depend only on $\tau$
(i.e. to 0th order in perturbation theory):
\begin{equation}
\odv{u}{\tau} = f(u, \tau),
\qquad
u(\tau_\ini) = u_\ini.
\label{eq:bg_ode}
\end{equation}
This only has to be done once if all parameters directly determine the initial conditions (i.e. an initial value problem (IVP)).
In some cases, the parameters determine a mixed set of initial and final conditions (i.e. a boundary value problem (BVP)),
and the background solve is wrapped inside the \textbf{shooting method} to find the initial conditions that produce the desired final conditions.
For most models (but not necessarily in general) the background ODEs decouple into blocks that can be solved sequentially,
such as the expansion history for $\calH(\tau)$ followed by thermodynamics for $x_e(\tau)$.
The background ODEs are usually quite small with 1-10 ODE variables,
but can be highly \textbf{stiff} due to the recombination and thermodynamics (see \cref{sec:approx}).
\label{eb:bg}

\item \textbf{Perturbation ODEs} are integrated in $\tau$ for variables $u(\tau, k)$ that depend on both time $\tau$ and wavenumber $k$
(i.e. to 1st order in perturbation theory):
\begin{equation}
\odv{u}{\tau} = f(u, \tau, k),
\qquad
u(\tau_\ini, k) = u_\ini.
\label{eq:pt_ode}
\end{equation}
The perturbations must usually be integrated for 100s of $k$-modes to resolve the $k$-dependence.
Importantly, linear perturbations in Fourier space are independent (see \cref{sec:fourier}),
so the same ODE system can be evolved independently for each $k$ and thus be parallelized (instead of solving for all $k$-modes together in a $100\times$ larger ODE).
The perturbation system for each $k$ usually consists of 10-100s of equations if only photons and massless neutrinos are included (with $\lmax \lesssim 10\text{-}100$),
and 100s-1000s of equations if massive neutrinos are also included (with similar $\lmax$ and $N_q \approx 5$ for massive neutrinos).
The perturbation equations are both \textbf{large} and highly \textbf{stiff},
particularly at early times,
which makes them hard to solve numerically (see \cref{sec:approx}).
It is usually the most expensive part of the computation.
\label{eb:pt}

\item \textbf{Line-of-sight integrals} are solved for multipoles today.
The CMB power spectrum needs the photon hierarchy $F_l(\tau_0,k)$ to very high $l \lesssim 3000$,
but evolving the perturbation ODEs with $\lmax \lesssim 3000$ is extremely expensive.
Instead, \cite{zaldarriagaAnalyticApproachPolarization1995,seljakLineSightApproach1996}
found an integral solution to equation \eqref{eq:boltzmann_linear} in the relativistic case \eqref{eq:Fl}.
Essentially, $F(\tau, k, \mu)$ is expressed as an integral \emph{before} it is expanded in multipoles $F_l(\tau,k)$,
which is the reverse order of operations compared to how the infinite $l$-hierarchy \eqref{eq:hierarchy_l} was derived for the ODEs.
It leads to a general method where multipoles $\Delta_l^A$ (such as $F_l$)
are computed as integrals of a source function $S^A(\tau,k)$ of the perturbations
weighted by spherical Bessel functions $j_l(x)$:
\begin{equation}
\Delta_l^A(k) = \int_{\tau_i}^{\tau_0} \diff{\tau}\,S^A(\tau,k)\,j_l(k(\tau_0-\tau)) .
\label{eq:los}
\end{equation}
The major advantage is that $S^A(\tau,k)$ depends on the perturbations only up to $l \leq 2$
and only weakly on their $\lmax$-truncation.
Thus, the perturbation ODEs can be solved with a small $\lmax$ that gives a good enough $S^A(\tau,k)$,
and then the integrals for $\Delta_l^A(k)$ can be evaluated for many $(k,l)$ by numerical quadrature,
which is much cheaper than solving ODEs.

\item \textbf{Power spectra}:
The matter power spectrum $P(k)$ is output directly from the solutions to the perturbation ODEs.
The CMB power spectrum must integrate the line-of-sight transfer functions over $k$:
\begin{equation}
C_l^{AB} \propto \int \diff{k}\,P_0(k)\,k^2\,\Delta_l^A(k)\,\Delta_l^B(k) .
\label{eq:Cl}
\end{equation}

\textbf{Interpolation} is a crucial operation \emph{between} each step of the pipeline (and of the final spectra).
The perturbation ODEs step in time independently of the background solution and must therefore query it at arbitrary $\tau$,
so the background solution is splined and provided as interpolated input to the perturbations.
The line-of-sight integrals $\Delta_l(k)$ oscillate rapidly in $k$
and must be computed on a much denser $k$-grid than what is required resolve $S^A(\tau,k)$ from the perturbations,
so the perturbations are solved on a coarser $k$-grid and used to interpolate $S^A(\tau, k)$ to a finer $k$-grid for line-of-sight integration.
The final power spectra in either $k$ or $l$ are wanted at arbitrary values,
so they are computed for as few $k$ and $l$ as possible and then interpolated.
Interpolation is the topic of \cref{paper:chebyshev},
which shows that the interpolation in $k$ and $l$ can be improved by using Chebyshev polynomials
instead of cubic splines, which is the standard method used in most other codes.
I also discuss this further in \cref{sec:spectral}.

Other post-processing steps of \EB{} codes can include \enquote{boosting} in order to incorporate nonlinear effects,
which is implemented in some \EB{} codes using halo modeling tools calibrated to $N$-body simulations
(e.g. Halofit \cite{smithStableClusteringHalo2003b,takahashiRevisingHalofitModel2012a}
and HMCODE \cite{meadAccurateHaloModel2015,meadAccurateHalomodelMatter2016,meadHMcode2020ImprovedModelling2021a}).
I reiterate, however, that the core task of an \EB{} remains the integration of the background, perturbations and line-of-sight integrals presented above.

Several \EB{} codes have been developed over the last three decades.
Some are still actively developed,
while others are abandoned or
have grown into other codes that have succeeded them.
In \cref{fig:codes} and below, I summarize the historical literature of \EB{} codes
and highlight the different designs and new advances made with each code.

\subsubsection{COSMICS}
COSMICS\footnote{\url{https://web.archive.org/web/19961122200110/http://arcturus.mit.edu/cosmics/}} \cite{maCosmologicalPerturbationTheory1995a,bertschingerCOSMICSCosmologicalInitial1995}
is the \enquote{Big Bang} of \EB{} codes.
It is the first major code
that includes general relativity, cold dark matter, massless and massive neutrinos, photons and baryons with a simplified treatment of recombination physics
in both the Newtonian and synchronous gauges.
This seminal work remains the standard reference for the systematic treatment of linear cosmological perturbation theory to this date,
and has standardized notation and conventions for later codes.
The code was written in Fortran and was later succeeded by CMBFAST.

\subsubsection{CMBFAST}
CMBFAST\footnote{\url{https://web.archive.org/web/20050308195517/cfa-www.harvard.edu/~mzaldarr/CMBFAST/cmbfast.html}} \cite{seljakLineSightApproach1996}
descends from COSMICS and is the code that introduced the line-of-sight integration method \eqref{eq:los}.
This sped up the computation by two orders of magnitude at without significant accuracy loss,
because it allows $\lmax \approx 10$ instead of $\lmax \approx 1500$ in the perturbation ODEs for each $k$,
and because the perturbation ODEs can be solved on a coarser $k$-grid before interpolation of the source function $S(\tau, k)$.
It was first made for flat universes,
but extended to open universes in \cite{zaldarriagaIntegralSolutionMicrowave1998}
and to closed universes in \cite{zaldarriagaCMBFASTSpatiallyClosed2000}.
It was also updated to include the effects of gravitational lensing on the CMB power spectra \cite{zaldarriagaGravitationalLensingEffect1998},
and was used in the first WMAP analysis \cite{verdeFirstYearWilkinson2003,spergelFirstYearWilkinson2003} of CMB data.
The code later evolved into CAMB and CMBEASY.

\subsubsection{CAMB}
CAMB\footnote{\url{https://camb.info/} and \url{https://github.com/cmbant/CAMB}} \cite{lewisEfficientComputationCMB2000a,lewisCAMBV2Cosmological2026a}
is built on CMBFAST and is one of the two most established codes in the cosmological community today, alongside CLASS.
It reimplemented CMBFAST in a new \enquote{1+3 covariant formalism} that evolves gauge-independent covariant variables relative to some four-velocity \cite{challinorCosmicMicrowaveBackground1999}.
Thus, CAMB's conventions departs somewhat from the gauge-dependent formalism in \cite{maCosmologicalPerturbationTheory1995a}.
Its first release targeted the implementation of closed geometries in the line-of-sight integral,
and appeared on arXiv only one day before the corresponding extension of CMBFAST \cite{lewisEfficientComputationCMB2000a,zaldarriagaCMBFASTSpatiallyClosed2000}.
CAMB was integrated with the parameter extraction code CosmoMC \cite{lewisCosmologicalParametersCMB2002},
which popularized MCMC pipelines where the output of \EB{} codes is compared with observations.
CAMB uses an explicit ODE integrator,
which makes it necessary to treat stiffness in the equations with analytical approximation schemes.
%The code recently received substantial updates developed with agentic AI under human supervision,
%including an approximation of hyperspherical Bessel functions that gradually reduce to
%Notably, CAMB uses an explicit ODE integrator,
%which makes it necessary to use approximation schemes for the stiff equations that are tuned for the \LCDM{} model.
%These characteristics make CAMB very efficient for the standard model,
%at the cost of being more difficult to extend to some modified models (see \cref{sec:approx}).
The core of CAMB remains written in Fortran,
but it also features a Python wrapper for accessing results
and modifying dark energy models without touching internal code.
CAMB is the most mature \EB{} code with ongoing active development from its over 30 year old roots.
It has also spawned several forks for extended models (see \cref{sec:modifications}).

\subsubsection{CMBEASY}
CMBEASY\footnote{\url{https://web.archive.org/web/20080105133830/http://cmbeasy.org/}} \cite{doranCMBEASYObjectOriented2005a,doranSpeedingCosmologicalBoltzmann2005b}
is a port of CMBFAST to C++.
It reorganized the code with object-oriented programming features
in order to facilitate modularity and simpler substitution of cosmological models, different gauge choices and so on,
and featured a graphical user interface to use the code.
For example, the object-oriented design was used to implement a variety of quintessence models for dark energy.
It was also extended with a package for MCMC parameter estimation \cite{doranAnalyzeThisCosmological2004}.
It is no longer under active development,
but its philosophy of structuring the code to allow for simple substitution of different submodels has influenced the design of later codes,
such as ABCMB and SymBoltz.

\subsubsection{CLASS}
CLASS\footnote{\url{http://class-code.net/} and \url{https://github.com/lesgourg/class_public/}} \cite{lesgourguesCosmicLinearAnisotropy2011d,blasCosmicLinearAnisotropy2011c,lesgourguesCosmicLinearAnisotropy2011e,lesgourguesCosmicLinearAnisotropy2011f}
is the other most established code in use today, alongside CAMB.
It is the first major code that is not from the COSMICS branch
and thus works as an independent cross-check of CAMB.
The two agree to $0.01\%$ with high-precision settings \cite{lesgourguesCosmicLinearAnisotropy2011e}.
CLASS was made to be more flexible and simpler to modify than CAMB and CMBFAST,
and its modules $\texttt{input} \to \texttt{background} \to \texttt{thermodynamics} \to \texttt{perturbations} \to \ldots \to \texttt{output}$
directly mirrors the computational structure of the \EB{} problem in \cref{sec:eb_codes}.
It offers very fine-grained control for tuning approximation schemes and precision parameters
in order to control the precision-performance tradeoff.
It is also the first code that makes it possible to disable some approximation schemes
thanks to an \emph{implicit} \texttt{ndf15} ODE integrator,
which evaluates the ODE Jacobian numerically
and finds its sparsity pattern to speed up linear algebra operations.
This makes it competitive with explicit integrators used by earlier codes.
It also features a generic implementation of the $(q,l)$-hierarchy \eqref{eq:hierarchy_ql}
for arbitrary distribution functions $f_0(q)$.
%It supports both the synchronous and Newtonian gauges.
It has been updated with algorithms for computing hyperspherical generalizations of the Bessel functions
that are needed for line-of-sight integration in closed universes,
using stable recurrence relations in $l$ instead of ODEs in $\tau$
\cite{lesgourguesFastAccurateCMB2014b,tramComputationHypersphericalBessel2017b}.
CLASS is written in C, and also provides a wrapper for use with Python.
It has been extended into a wide range of modified models and additional computations (see \cref{sec:modifications}).
%SONG for second order perturbation theory,
%CLASS-PT for modeling mildly nonlinear structure formation with the effective field theory of large-scale structure,
%and ExoCLASS for exotic energy injection (see \cref{sec:modifications}).

\subsubsection{PyCosmo}
PyCosmo\footnote{\url{https://pypi.org/project/PyCosmo}} \cite{refregierPyCosmoIntegratedCosmological2017a,tarsitanoPredictingCosmologicalObservables2020a,moserSymbolicImplementationExtensions2021}
is a third independent code and the first to be written primarily in Python,
which is a very widely used programming language in cosmology today.
It is the first code to feature a symbolic interface for specifying equations
to make it simpler for users to implement modified models.
The symbolic equations are specified with \texttt{sympy} and then compiled to efficient C/C++ code for the numerical solver,
using the utility \texttt{sympy2c} \cite{schmittSympy2cSymbolicExpressions2022a} that was made in tandem with the code.
It is also the first code that is primarily designed to be completely free of approximation schemes:
the symbolic equations are used to derive the ODE Jacobian \emph{analytically} in sparse form,
which is then provided to an implicit \texttt{BDF2} ODE solver (see also \cref{sec:approx}).
With this coupling of a symbolic framework and implicit solvers,
PyCosmo is competitive with CLASS in terms of both performance and precision.

\subsubsection{Bolt}
Bolt\footnote{\url{https://github.com/xzackli/Bolt.jl/}} \cite{XzackliBoltjlCitation}
is the first differentiable \EB{} code and is the first code written in Julia.
It can compute gradients of the output, such as matter and CMB power spectra,
with forward-mode automatic differentiation using ForwardDiff.jl \cite{revelsForwardModeAutomaticDifferentiation2016} (see \cref{sec:differentiability}).
Bolt is compatible with the very comprehensive OrdinaryDiffEq.jl library of ODE solvers \cite{rackauckasDifferentialEquationsjlPerformantFeatureRich2017a}.
This makes it straightforward to swap out the numerical solver by changing only one line of code,
which is very important with stiff ODEs, where the optimal solver is highly problem-dependent (see \cref{sec:approx}).
Older codes include only one or two built-in ODE solvers and do not have this ability.
At the time of writing, the code has not been actively developed since 2024 and has no accompanying paper.

\subsubsection{DISCO-EB}
DISCO-EB\footnote{\url{https://github.com/ohahn/DISCO-EB/}} \cite{hahnDISCODJDifferentiableEinsteinBoltzmann2024a}
is another differentiable \EB{} code written in the JAX framework in Python \cite{jax}.
This is a popular framework that extends the \texttt{numpy} library in Python with support for automatic differentiation and GPUs, for example.
It is the first \EB{} code that targets GPU compatibility,
which can parallelize the evaluation of independent perturbation ODEs \eqref{eq:pt_ode},
line-of-sight integrals \eqref{eq:los}
and entire parameter sets to a greater extent than CPUs.
GPU solvers like straightforward and branch-free computations,
so DISCO-EB uses implicit solvers to avoid approximation schemes.
DISCO-EB is compatible with the Diffrax ODE solver library \cite{kidgerNeuralDifferentialEquations2022},
which also offers an appreciable suite of implicit ODE solvers similarly to OrdinaryDiffEq.jl in Bolt.
At the time of writing, development has focused on enabling a fully differentiable end-to-end simulation pipeline from linear perturbation theory through nonlinear $N$-body simulations of structure formation \cite{hahnDISCODJDifferentiableEinsteinBoltzmann2024a,listDISCODJIIDifferentiable2026}.
Consequently, DISCO-EB currently has a simplified recombination treatment and does not compute CMB observables, for example.

\subsubsection{ABCMB}
ABCMB\footnote{\url{https://github.com/TonyZhou729/ABCMB}} \cite{zhouABCMBPythonJAXPackage2026b} is
another differentiable \EB{} code.
It is built on the same Python+JAX platform as DISCO-EB and also uses Diffrax,
but also features detailed implementations of recombination, BBN, line-of-sight integration and lensed CMB power spectra.
Like DISCO-EB, it targets GPU parallelization, avoids approximation schemes
and reports better runtime scaling with increasing $\lmax$ over CPU-based codes.
For the whole pipeline to support automatic differentiation,
ABCMB cannot simply call out to external recombination codes (like CAMB and CLASS can),
but reimplements HyRec-2 in native JAX (see \cref{sec:modify_recombination}).
Similarly to CMBEASY, it features an object-oriented interface for adding new species
by specifying their energy-momentum contributions \eqref{eq:totalT} as a class,
which makes it convenient to implement modifications when they fit into this form.

\subsubsection{SymBoltz}
SymBoltz\footnote{\url{https://github.com/hersle/SymBoltz.jl/}} \cite{sletmoenSymBoltzjlSymbolicnumericApproximationfree2026}
brings together what in my opinion are the successes of the newer \EB{} codes.
Like PyCosmo, SymBoltz features a symbolic interface for specifying equations that is built on ModelingToolkit.jl \cite{maModelingToolkita},
but takes the idea further to expose all variables, parameters, ordinary and algebraic equations and initial conditions
of the entire \EB{} system \eqref{eq:eb} through one consistent symbolic interface.
This makes it equally simple to modify any part of the equations.
Furthermore, the \enquote{knowledge} of the symbolic equations is used to automate as many tasks as possible,
such as separation of stages, interpolation of arbitrary variables, generation of numerical ODE code, specification of initial conditions and setup of the shooting method.
Like PyCosmo, SymBoltz generates analytical and sparse ODE Jacobians from the symbolic equations
and passes them to implicit ODE solvers,
in order to avoid approximations while remaining performant.
Like Bolt, it is compatible with OrdinaryDiffEq.jl for testing many different implicit ODE solvers,
and uses the implicit \texttt{Rodas5P} solver by default which avoids Newton iterations.
%The symbolic-numeric structure is not custom-built but uses ModelingToolkit.jl,
%which is an independently developed library that continuously receives improvements
%and is tightly integrated with the OrdinaryDiffEq.jl ODE library.
%The development of SymBoltz has contributed significantly back to both libraries.
Like Bolt, DISCO-EB and ABCMB, SymBoltz is compatible with forward-mode automatic differentiation
for computing accurate gradients without tuning finite difference step sizes.
%This works through the implicit ODE solvers and the symbolic interface,
%but it requires that SymBoltz solves the full \EB{} equations without calling out to external codes.
%This makes SymBoltz reimplements recombination models inside its symbolic interface,
%while most other codes treat these as external programs and makes it challenging to support automatic differentiation.
SymBoltz also features interpolation in $k$ and $l$ using Chebyshev polynomials,
which can converge faster than cubic splines used in other codes
due to the smooth output of the approximation-free equations.
Like Bolt, SymBoltz is written in the Julia programming language,
which strives to resolve the two-language problem that is prevalent in cosmology \cite{bezansonJuliaFreshApproach2015a},
(as seen by the common need of creating Python wrappers around C and Fortran codes).
The design of SymBoltz is elaborated upon in \cref{sec:symboltz}.

\subsection{Dark energy models}
\label{sec:modify_de}

Our understanding of the cosmological constant is poor,
and there is no good reason why dark energy could not have a dynamical and time-dependent nature.
There is a very large range of modified dark energy models \cite[e.g.][]{copelandDynamicsDarkEnergy2006}.

Dark energy can be modeled as a fluid (\cref{sec:fluid}) with a general and time-dependent equation of state $w$
(the cosmological constant corresponds to $w = -1$).
A very popular parameterization is the Chevallier--Polarski--Linder equation of state
\begin{equation}
    w(a) = w_0 + w_a (1-a)
\label{eq:w0wa}
\end{equation}
for which the continuity equation \eqref{eq:fluid_continuity_bg} admits the new time-dependent analytical solution
$\rho_\Lambda(a) = \rho_\Lambda(1) \, a^{-3(1+w_0+w_a)} e^{-3 w_a (1-a)}$
\cite{chevallierAcceleratingUniversesScaling2001,linderExploringExpansionHistory2003}.
Several other parameterizations and reconstruction of the full $w(a)$ from data have also been proposed,
all of which can be used to probe for deviations from the cosmological constant
\cite[e.g.][]{jassalWMAPConstraintsLow2005,sahniReconstructingDarkEnergy2006,seikelReconstructionDarkEnergy2012,hutererDarkEnergyTwo2018}.
%such as a constant $w = w_0$ with only one parameter,
%$w(a) = w_0 + w_a(a_p-a)$ around a general pivot scale factor $a_p$,
%$w(z) = w_0 + w_1 z / (1+z)^p$ for $p \in \{1,2\}$ in redshift $z$,
%similar parameterizations for the density $\rho_\Lambda(a)$,
%piecewise constant (\enquote{binned}) $w(z) = w_i$ for $z_{i-1} < z \leq z_{i}$
%and reconstruction of the full $w(a)$ from data using Gaussian processes
%\cite[e.g.][]{jassalWMAPConstraintsLow2005,sahniReconstructingDarkEnergy2006,seikelReconstructionDarkEnergy2012,hutererDarkEnergyTwo2018}.
The Chaplygin gas
is a unified dark energy and dark matter fluid with equation of state $P=-A/\rho$,
which gives the continuity equation \eqref{eq:fluid_continuity_bg} the analytical solution $\rho = \sqrt{A+B/a^6}$
that decays like dark matter at early times but stays constant at late times
\cite{kamenshchikAlternativeQuintessence2001}.

Dark energy can also be described by a scalar field $\varphi$ that rolls in a freely specified potential function $V(\varphi)$.
Quintessence is the best known class of such models
\cite{ratraCosmologicalConsequencesRolling1988,caldwellCosmologicalImprintEnergy1998,tsujikawaQuintessenceReview2013}.
Here the scalar field follows a \KG{} equation
\begin{equation}
\ddot{\varphi} = -2\calH\dot{\varphi} - a^2 V^\prime(\varphi)
\label{eq:kg}
\end{equation}
and contributes $\rho = \dot{\varphi}^2 / 2 a^2 + V(\varphi)$ and $P = \dot{\varphi}^2 / 2 a^2 - V(\varphi)$.
If $V(\varphi)$ is chosen so $\varphi$ rolls slowly, then $w \rightarrow -1$.
A similar \KG{} equation must be solved in the perturbations.
Scalar fields have been used to build Early Dark Energy (EDE) models,
which speed up expansion in the early universe to lower the sound horizon
while preserving the late-time evolution
in order to alleviate the $H_0$ tension
\cite[e.g.][]{doranEarlyDarkEnergy2006,kamionkowskiDarkEnergyString2014,poulinCosmologicalImplicationsUltralight2018,poulinEarlyDarkEnergy2019,kamionkowskiHubbleTensionEarly2022,poulinUpsDownsEarly2023}.
Quintessence can also be generalized to larger classes of models,
such as $k$-essence and Horndeski theories (\cref{sec:modify_gravity}).

These models require modified or new background and perturbation equations
(the cosmological constant has no perturbations)
and additional parameters and initial conditions.

\subsection{Dark matter models}
\label{sec:modify_dm}

One plausible explanation for dark matter is that it is some elementary particle that we have not yet discovered,
and that the cold dark matter equations \eqref{eq:eb_cold_dark_matter} are simply good approximations to it on cosmological scales.
One example is axions,
which are ultralight particles that can mimic both dark matter and dark energy
\cite[e.g.][]{marshAxionCosmology2016}.
They are implemented in the modified \EB{} solvers
AxionCAMB \cite{hlozekSearchUltralightAxions2015} and
AxiCLASS \cite{poulinCosmologicalImplicationsUltralight2018}.
Here a massive scalar field $\varphi$ must be evolved in a \KG{} equation
$\ddot{\varphi} + 2 \calH \dot\varphi + a^2 m^2 \varphi = 0$ in the background.
In the perturbations, the \KG{} equations are oscillatory, stiff and expensive to solve exactly,
but its energy-momentum contributions can be mapped exactly onto
a generalized dark matter (GDM) fluid description \cite{huStructureFormationGeneralized1998},
which is easier to evolve in time.
Depending on their mass $m$,
Axions mimics dark energy at early times when $H \gg m$ with slow rolling,
and transitions to dark matter at late times when $H \lesssim m$ and the field starts to oscillate around the minimum of the potential.

Non-cold dark matter (NCDM) models
relax the assumptions that dark matter is cold (CDM) with non-relativistic velocity and negligible velocity dispersion.
For example, massive neutrinos are non-relativistic and make up a small fraction of the dark matter content today,
but were relativistic in the early universe and thus a form of hot dark matter (HDM).
CLASS has an elegant implementation of non-cold dark matter models
that reads an arbitrary distribution function $f(q)$,
either specified analytically or interpolated from a data table.
It then automatically uses $f(q)$ to determine quadrature points $q_i$ and weights $W_i$
for the general $(q,l)$-hierarchy \eqref{eq:eb_massive_neutrinos}
(which is valid for general $f(q)$; massive neutrinos are just a special case with $f(q) = 1/(e^{q/T}+1)$).
For example, this framework has been used to study NCDM models from
evaporation of primordial black holes \cite{baldesNonColdDarkMatter2020},
sterile neutrinos \cite{venumadhavSterileNeutrinoDark2016,schneiderAstrophysicalConstraintsResonantly2016}
and from a variety of out-of-equilibrium decay and scattering processes \cite{ballesterosHowWarmAre2021}.

Decaying dark matter is another class of models,
where dark matter gradually decays into some species.
For example, some models assume that dark matter (a fluid with $w=0$) decays with rate $\Gamma$
into an unknown \enquote{dark radiation} (DR) component (a relativistic fluid with $w=1/3$ which is neither photons nor neutrinos)
\cite[e.g]{audrenStrongestModelindependentBound2014a,poulinFreshLookLinear2016,bringmannConvertingNonrelativisticDark2018}.
This requires new and modified continuity equations
\begin{equation}
\dot\rho_\mathrm{DM} = -3\calH \rho_\mathrm{DM} - a \, \Gamma \rho_\mathrm{DM},
\qquad
\dot\rho_\mathrm{DR} = -4\calH \rho_\mathrm{DR} + a \, \Gamma \rho_\mathrm{DM}.
\end{equation}
The perturbed continuity and Euler equations require similar modifications,
and another full $l$-hierarchy \eqref{eq:hierarchy_l} must be evolved for the dark radiation species.
If the decay involves a visible component such as photons or baryons,
it can also affect recombination and reionization \cite[e.g.][]{chenParticleDecaysCosmic2004}
and must be treated with \enquote{exotic injection} into the recombination codes (see \cref{sec:modify_baryons}).
In general, this couples the background and thermodynamics physics,
which are hardcoded to be distinct sequential stages of the background equations.

Such a decaying type of dark matter is an example of interacting dark matter models,
of which there are many other examples (see \cref{sec:modify_interacting}).

\subsection{Recombination, reionization and baryon physics}
\label{sec:modify_baryons}

The rate of change $\dot{x}_e$ of the free electron fraction in the baryon equations \eqref{eq:eb_baryons}
is determined by recombination and reionization physics.
The CMB power spectrum needs accurate modeling particularly of recombination,
which has been increasingly refined over the last decades.
Recombination is often outsourced to one of several \enquote{recombination codes},
but in my opinion it should be understood as a part of the \EB{} system \eqref{eq:eb}
on equal footing with other equations.

\textbf{Peebles' equation} gives the basic recombination picture established by \cite{peeblesRecombinationPrimevalPlasma1968,zeldovichRecombinationHydrogenHot1968}.
It is an effective three-state model of Hydrogen with
the continuum of ions and electrons ($n=\infty$),
the ground state 1s ($n=1$),
and all excited states assumed to be in equilibrium with 2s and 2p ($n=2$).
Direct $1 \leftrightarrow \infty$ transitions have no net effect and are ignored,
as combination directly to the ground state emits a photon with $E \geq 13.6\,\mathrm{eV}$ that immediately reionizes a nearby atom and cancels the effect.
Peebles' equation therefore describes the rate of change of the \Hy{} ionization fraction $x_\Hy = n_{\Hy^+} / n_\Hy$
only through the two transitions $1 \leftrightarrow 2 \leftrightarrow \infty$ \cite{maCosmologicalPerturbationTheory1995a}:
\begin{equation}
\dot{x}_\Hy = -a \, C_r \left[ \alpha^{(2)}(T_b) \, n_\Hy \, x_e \, x_\Hy - \beta(T_\gamma) (1-x_\Hy) \right].
\label{eq:peebles}
\end{equation}
In brackets is the net rate of combination from $n=\infty$ to $n=2$,
which depends on the densities of $e^-$, $\Hy{}^+$ and neutral \Hy{},
baryon temperature $T_b$ and photon temperature $T_\gamma$.
This is multiplied by the reduction factor $C_r$,
which describes the probability of decaying $2 \to 1$ instead of reionizing $2 \to \infty$.
This is necessary because the ground state can only be reached through one of two \enquote{bottlenecked} decays:
$2p \to 1s$ if the emitted Lyman-$\alpha$ photon redshifts to a larger wavelength before it has time to re-excite $1s \to 2p$ (\enquote{Sobolev escape}),
or $2s \to 1s$ with two-photon emission.
In contrast, the path $2s \to 1s$ with one-photon emission is prohibited because it violates the selection rule $\Delta l = \pm 1$.
Peebles' model describes all these processes \emph{effectively}
without invoking all details of atomic physics and resolving all the atomic states.
It is the recombination model that was implemented in COSMICS \cite{maCosmologicalPerturbationTheory1995a},
but it is not accurate enough for modern data.

\textbf{RECFAST} \cite{seagerNewCalculationRecombination1999b}
refines Peebles' effective model.
Most notably, it adds some free \enquote{fudge factors} to it,
and fits them to reproduce the output of an expensive multi-level simulation of the detailed atomic recombination physics
(using hundreds of states of $\Hy$, $\Hy^+$, $\He$, $\He^+$, $\He^{++}$, $e^-$, $\Hy^-$, $\Hy_2$ and $\Hy_2^+$).
This is then compressed into Peebles-like equations \eqref{eq:peebles} for $\Hy \leftrightarrow \Hy^+$ and $\He \leftrightarrow \He^+$ and Saha's equation for $\He^+ \leftrightarrow \He^{++}$,
but with a recombination coefficient $\alpha_\Hy(T_b) \to F \cdot \alpha_\Hy(T_b)$ fudged by the empirical number $F=1.14$.
In this way, it combines the accuracy of the multi-level computation with the speed of Peebles' equation,
but the fitting assumes a fiducial \LCDM{} model and is less trustworthy for extended models.
RECFAST has been updated and \enquote{re-fudged} several times since the original version
to account for more effects and reproduce newer and increasingly accurate recombination results
%for example with corrections to \He{} recombination through distinct singlet and triplet channels,
%and by running multi-level simulations that resolve both $(n,l)$ instead of just $n$.
\cite{wongHowWellWe2007,rubino-martinEstimatingImpactRecombination2010,chlubaRadiativeTransferEffects2012a,collaborationPlanck2013Results2014,lewisCAMBV2Cosmological2026a}.
Its ad-hoc nature has motivated development of more physically principled recombination models,
but RECFAST remains the default recombination model in CAMB
and has been re-calibrated to the output of CosmoRec (see below)
\cite{lewisCAMBV2Cosmological2026a}.

\textbf{CosmoRec} \cite{chlubaCompleteTreatmentCosmological2010} and
\textbf{HyRec} \cite{ali-haimoudHyRecFastHighly2011a}
are more physically principled recombination codes that avoid the fudge factors of RECFAST.
They both use a new method called the effective multi-level atom (EMLA) \cite{ali-haimoudUltrafastEffectiveMultilevel2010}
for treating the transitions between atomic states.
As explained in \cref{fig:emla}, the EMLA splits the excited atomic states into \enquote{interior} and \enquote{interface} states.
Its central result is that the effect of the \enquote{interior states}
can be accounted for \emph{exactly} by effective combination and ionization coefficients $A_i(T_b, T_\gamma)$ and $B_i(T_\gamma)$
to the \enquote{interface states}.
These coefficients depend on $T_b$ and $T_\gamma$ but \emph{not on cosmology},
so they can be precomputed once with an expensive simulation.
The problem then reduces to looking up $A_i$ and $B_i$ with interpolation,
and tracking occupation fractions $x_i$ for only a few cosmology-dependent \enquote{interface states} $i$,
at most $\{2s, 2p, 3s, 3p, 3d, 4s, 4p, 4d\}$:
\begin{equation}
\dot{x}_e = -\sum_i \big( n_\Hy x_e^2 A_i(T_b, T_\gamma) - x_i B_i(T_\gamma) \big) .
\label{eq:hyrec}
\end{equation}
These codes also treat the photon field more carefully
by solving for its intensity distribution $J(\tau, \nu)$ both in time and frequency,
instead of using Peebles' simplified reduction factor with the Sobolev approximation.
This is a performance bottleneck that makes HyRec and CosmoRec slower than RECFAST,
although they are more accurate and physically principled.
%In some sense, the EMLA is a definitive and elegant exact generalization of Peebles' original three-level atom.
%This elegantly ends the recombination problem by circling back to the original treatments and solving them exactly.

\textbf{HyRec-2} \cite{leeHYREC2HighlyAccurate2020}
resolves this performance bottleneck.
Starting with an approximate base model of the ground state transition rate
similar to Peebles' $2s \to 1s$ and $2p \to 1s$ treatment,
HyRec-2 adds on top a tabulated correction function from an accurate (but slower) full HyRec simulation.
This is similar to the fudge factors in RECFAST and sacrifices some generality,
but it is far less severe because it is applied on top of a more accurate base model,
and the correction function also includes derivatives around the Planck 2018 parameters.
With the EMLA and this trick, HyRec-2 avoids solving for the frequency-dependence of the photon field,
and is both more accurate and faster than RECFAST.

\textbf{Reionization} models modify the late-time ionization history of the universe,
when radiation from the first stars and galaxies reionize the neutral atoms after recombination.
It is difficult to describe these processes from first principles with linear theory,
so many reionization models come in parameterized forms, such as
instantaneous step function models \cite{seljakLineSightApproach1996,bruscoliHowReionizationEpoch2002},
piecewise binned models \cite{lewisCosmicMicrowaveBackground2006},
a smooth and redshift-symmetric $\tanh$-activated function \cite{lewisCosmologicalParametersWMAP2008a}
\begin{equation}
x_e(z) = \frac{f}{2}\left[1 + \tanh \left(\frac{y_{\rm re} - y}{\Delta y}\right)\right], \qquad y = (1+z)^{3/2} ,
\end{equation}
and redshift-asymmetric exponential models \cite{douspisNewParameterizationReionisation2015,collaborationPlanckIntermediateResults2016}
%\begin{equation}
%x_e = \begin{cases} 1-a^{-3} & \text{if $a<a_p$} \\ e^{-\lambda/a} & \text{if $a \geq a_p$} \end{cases}
%\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
x_e(z) = f \left(\frac{z_{\rm early} - z}{z_{\rm early} - z_{\rm end}}\right)^{\alpha} .
\end{equation}
and model-independent principal component analyses \cite{huModelIndependentReionizationObservables2003,mortonsonReionizationConstraintsFiveyear2008,heinrichCompleteReionizationConstraints2017a}.
A different type of models inject additional \enquote{exotic energy} from non-standard models into the differential baryon equations \eqref{eq:eb_baryons},
which are modified in the schematic form
\cite{poulinDarkMatterAnnihilations2015,stockerExoticEnergyInjection2018}
\begin{equation}
\dot{x}_e \rightarrow \dot{x}_e + I_X,
\qquad
\dot{T}_b \rightarrow \dot{T}_b + \,K_h .
\end{equation}
The injections $I_X$ and $K_h$ come from non-standard models,
for example decaying and annihilating dark matter or evaporation of light primordial black holes.

\textbf{Other baryon modifications}
have implemented perturbed effects of recombination \cite{lewisLinearEffectsPerturbed2007}.
It has also been pointed out by \cite{pookkillathBaryonPhysicsTight2019} that the baryon and general fluid equations
in \cite[equations 29-30 vs. 66-67]{maCosmologicalPerturbationTheory1995a}
(our equations \eqref{eq:eb_baryons} vs. \eqref{eq:fluid})
are inconsistent because every term involving baryon pressure is ignored \emph{except for} the term proportional to $(\delta P/\delta\rho) k^2 \delta$.
This breaks general covariance and energy-momentum conservation.
Similarly, one could argue that the nonzero baryon equation of state $w_b \ll 1$
makes it inconsistent to solve the background independently of thermodynamics.
In principle, the background continuity equation $\dot{\rho_b} = -3\calH(1+w_b) \rho_b$ with $w_b \neq 0$ from thermodynamics
should be evolved as one coupled system.
This would be hard to implement in most codes that are structured with the background being independent of thermodynamics,
by assuming $w_b=0$ so $\rho_b \propto a^{-3}$ is solved analytically and independently of thermodynamics.

\subsection{Modified gravity theories}
\label{sec:modify_gravity}

Just like Newton's laws of gravity and motion works great in many cases,
it could be the case that Einstein's theory of \GR{} is a limit or approximation to the true theory of gravity.
In principle, modified gravity theories also explain phenomena
such as galaxy rotation curves, enhanced clustering and the late-time acceleration
without dark matter and dark energy, which we do not understand the nature of anyway.\footnote{\url{https://xkcd.com/1758/}}
Several (classes of) modified gravity theories have bene implemented in \EB{} codes.

MGCAMB \cite{zhaoSearchingModifiedGrowth2009} and
MGCLASS \cite{bakerObservationalSignaturesModified2015,sakrCosmologicalConstraintsSubhorizon2022}
modify the perturbed Einstein equations \eqref{eq:eb_gravity}
with the phenomenological parameterizations
\begin{subequations}
\begin{align}
k^2\psi &= -4\pi \, \mu(a,k) \, a^2 \big( \rho \Delta + 3(\rho+P)\sigma \big), \label{eq:mg_poisson} \\
k^2\big(\phi - \gamma(a,k) \psi\big) &= 12\pi \mu(a,k) \, a^2 (\rho+P)\sigma \label{eq:mg_slip} .
\end{align}%
\label{eq:mg}%
\end{subequations}
with a freely chosen \enquote{gravitational coupling} $\mu(a,k)$ and \enquote{slip} $\eta(a, k)$.
Derivatives like $\dot\phi$ are still needed by the continuity equation \eqref{eq:pert_cont},
and must be found by differentiating the above expressions.

$\mathcal{H}$-EFTCAMB \cite{huEffectiveFieldTheory2014,raveriEffectiveFieldTheory2014,yeHEFTCAMBCobayaIntegratedPythonWrapped2026} and
hi\_class \cite{zumalacarreguiHiclassHorndeskiCosmic2017a,belliniHiclassBackgroundEvolution2020a}
implement general classes of modified gravity theories.
For example, they both support the popular class of Horndeski theories.
This is the most general class theories with second order equations of motion
for a metric tensor $g_{\mu\nu}$ and scalar field $\varphi$.
A Horndeski theory is specified by symbolic expressions for four free functions
$G_2(\varphi, X)$, $G_3(\varphi, X)$, $G_4(\varphi, X)$ and $G_5(\varphi, X)$
of $\varphi$ and the kinetic term $X = -\frac12(\partial_\mu \varphi)^2$ in its Lagrangian.
The background gets an additional \KG{} equation that must be solved together with modified Friedmann equations.
Notably, the 1st Friedmann equation can become a constraint equation that cannot be solved for $\calH$ directly,
so the acceleration equation must be integrated instead with \enquote{constraint damping} of the 1st equation in order to accurately satisfy both.
The perturbed Einstein equations are also modified and must be solved together with a perturbed \KG{} equation.
The coefficients in the perturbation equations are given in terms of four general functions
$\alpha_K(\tau)$, $\alpha_B(\tau)$, $\alpha_T(\tau)$ and $\alpha_M(\tau)$,
which have general expressions in terms of $G_i$ and its derivatives with respect to $\varphi$ and $X$
\cite{belliniMaximalFreedomMinimum2014a}.

Modified gravity theories introduce new and modified background and perturbation equations.
Symbolic work is frequently necessary to specify the equations
(e.g. turning expressions for $\psi$ and $\phi$ into $\dot\psi$ and $\dot\phi$,
or computation of $\alpha_i$ from $G_i$),
so integration with a symbolic library could make it easier to specify the theories from high-level equation input.
They also introduce additional stiffness in the \EB{} system,
which hi\_class overcomes with a new quasi-static approximation (QSA) scheme.

All species in the \LCDM{} model interact indirectly with each other through gravity,
but only photons and baryons interact directly through Thomson scattering.
Interactions between other species is thus a natural way to extend \LCDM{}.

In the language of the Boltzmann equation \eqref{eq:boltzmann},
interactions correspond to a collision term $C \neq 0$ on the right side.
In the language of energy-momentum conservation $\nabla_\mu T^{\mu\nu} = 0$
of the total $T_{\mu\nu} = \sum_a T_{\mu\nu}^a$ from every species $a$,
it corresponds to allowing a flow $Q^\nu_a \neq$ into every species'
\begin{equation}
\nabla_\mu T^{\mu\nu}_a = Q^\nu_a ,
\end{equation}
such that $\sum_a Q^\nu_a = 0$ (e.g. with $Q^\nu_a = \sum_b Q^\nu_{ab}$ with $Q^\nu_{ab} = -Q^\nu_{ba}$).

\Cref{tab:interactions} considers every pair of species in the \LCDM{} model
and dark radiation mentioned in \cref{sec:modify_dm},
and lists example works that have modified \EB{} codes to implement interactions between them.

Interactions between photons and axion-like scalar fields (a dark matter or energy candidate; see \cref{sec:modify_dm})
rotates the plane of linear polarization of photons as they travel through space
causes an effect called cosmic birefringence, and nonzero CMB power spectra $C_l^\mathrm{TB}$ and $C_l^\mathrm{EB}$,
which are zero in the standard model \cite{nakatsukaCosmicBirefringenceDue2022}.
Must add a new term to line-of-sight integration for polarization.

interactions between the fluids of baryons, dark matter and dark energy
are incorporated by additional collision terms $Q \neq 0$ on the right sides

\subsection{Time-dependent dark energy}

The cosmological constant is an \enquote{ad-hoc} modification of the Einstein equations
that effectively adds a time-independent density \eqref{eq:cc} to the energy-momentum.
Dark energy is mostly inferred from the accelerating late-time expansion,
but our understanding of it is poor and there is no fundamental theoretical argument for why it must have a time-independent nature.
Theoretical models and time-dependent parameterizations for dark energy are therefore a natural and popular class of \LCDM{} extensions.

\subsubsection{Dark energy parameterizations}

One approach is to parameterize the time-dependence of dark energy as a fluid (\cref{sec:fluid}).
A popular parameterization is the Chevallier--Polarski--Linder equation of state,
for which the background continuity equation \eqref{eq:fluid_continuity_bg} admits a new analytical solution
\cite{chevallierAcceleratingUniversesScaling2001,linderExploringExpansionHistory2003}:
\begin{equation}
w(a) = w_0 + w_a (1-a)
\quad \implies \quad
\rho_\Lambda(a) = \rho_\Lambda(1) \, a^{-3(1+w_0+w_a)} e^{-3 w_a (1-a)}.
\end{equation}
This is just a first-order Taylor expansion of the equation of state around $a=1$ with two free parameters $w_0$ and $w_a$.
New perturbation equations \eqref{eq:fluid} for $\delta$ and $\theta$ are also needed \cite[e.g.][]{putterMeasuringSpeedDark2010},
as the pure cosmological constant has no perturbations by definition.

Many other parameterizations have also been proposed, such as
a single-parameter $w = w_0$,
$w(a) = w_0 + w_a(a_p-a)$ around a general pivot scale factor $a_p$,
$w(z) = w_0 + w_1 z / (1+z)^p$ for $p \in \{1,2\}$ in redshift $z$,
similar parameterizations for the density $\rho_\Lambda(a)$,
piecewise constant (\enquote{binned}) $w(z) = w_i$ for $z_{i-1} < z \leq z_{i}$
and reconstruction of the full $w(a)$ from data using Gaussian processes
\cite[e.g.][]{jassalWMAPConstraintsLow2005,sahniReconstructingDarkEnergy2006,seikelReconstructionDarkEnergy2012,hutererDarkEnergyTwo2018}.

\subsubsection{Dark energy models}

A large class of models introduce a scalar field $\varphi$ (alongside the metric tensor $g_{\mu\nu}$)
that rolls in a freely specified potential function $V(\varphi)$ \cite[e.g.][]{copelandDynamicsDarkEnergy2006}.
Quintessence%
\footnote{\enquote{The fifth essence}, named in the hope of being the final contributor to $T_{\mu\nu}$ after baryons, dark matter, photons and neutrinos.}
\cite{ratraCosmologicalConsequencesRolling1988,caldwellCosmologicalImprintEnergy1998,tsujikawaQuintessenceReview2013}
is the purest and best known type,
where the kinetic term $X = -\frac12 (\nabla\varphi)^2$ and potential term $V(\varphi)$ enters the Lagrangian density in the canonical form $X - V(\varphi)$.
This results in a \KG{} equation that must be evolved for $\varphi$ in the background,
and the scalar field contributes to the total energy and pressure:
\begin{equation}
\ddot{\varphi} = -2\calH\dot{\varphi} - a^2 V^\prime(\varphi) , \qquad
\rho = \frac{\dot{\varphi}^2}{2 a^2} + V(\varphi) , \qquad
P = \frac{\dot{\varphi}^2}{2 a^2} - V(\varphi) .
\end{equation}
A \KG{} equation for perturbations $\delta\varphi$ must also be solved.

$k$-essence generalizes quintessence
to Lagrangian densities where $X$ and $\varphi$ enter in a general \enquote{non-canonical} way $p(X, \varphi)$.
It leads to modified \KG{} equations in the background and perturbations.
It is also a subclass of Horndeski theories,
which is a general class of modified gravity theories (see \cref{sec:modify_gravity}).

The Chaplygin gas \cite{kamenshchikAlternativeQuintessence2001}
treats dark energy not as a scalar field,
but as a fluid that transitions from dark matter to dark energy.
With the equation of state
\begin{equation}
P = -\frac{A}{\rho} ,
\end{equation}
the continuity equation \eqref{eq:fluid_continuity_bg} integrates analytically (without interactions) to
\begin{equation}
\rho = \sqrt{A + \frac{B}{a^6}} = \begin{cases} \sqrt{B} \, a^{-3} & \text{if $a \ll (B/A)^{1/6}$}, \\ \sqrt{A} & \text{if $a \gg (B/A)^{1/6}$} . \end{cases}
\end{equation}
Thus, the Chaplygin gas transitions from dark matter in the early universe
to dark energy similar to the cosmological constant in the late universe.
It is attractive because it unifies two poorly understood sectors of the \LCDM{} model.

Other dark energy models include Early Dark Energy \cite[e.g.][]{doranEarlyDarkEnergy2006,kamionkowskiDarkEnergyString2014,poulinCosmologicalImplicationsUltralight2018,poulinEarlyDarkEnergy2019,kamionkowskiHubbleTensionEarly2022,poulinUpsDownsEarly2023},
which is a scalar field that activates briefly in the early universe and then dies out.
Dark energy could also interact with other species (see \cref{sec:modify_interacting}).
Modifications of gravity, such as Horndeski theories,
also introduce scalar fields that play the role of dark energy and additionally modify the Einstein equations (see \cref{sec:modify_gravity}).

\subsection{Consistent baryon physics}
\label{sec:modify_baryons}

All standard \EB{} codes treat baryons inconsistently, as pointed out by \cite{pookkillathBaryonPhysicsTight2019} for example.
The seminal work of \cite[section 5.6]{maCosmologicalPerturbationTheory1995a} (rightfully) treat baryons as a non-relativistic fluid with $\delta P/\delta \rho = w \ll 1$.
They set $\delta P/\delta \rho = \omega$ in the continuity equation \eqref{eq:pert_cont},
but inconsistently set $w=\dot{w}=0$ and $\delta P/\delta \rho \neq w$ in the Euler equation \eqref{eq:fluid_euler}.
The $\delta P/\delta \rho$ is preserved only in the Euler equation because it is multiplied by $k^2$ there, and thus important at small scales.
This is a good approximation for the solution of the equations viewed from a purely numerical perspective,
but from a physical perspective it is inconsistent and breaks gauge-covariance and energy-momentum conservation $\nabla_\mu T^{\mu\nu} = 0$.

Even at the background level, it is common to assume that baryons have $w_b=0$ and evolve as $\rho_b \propto a^{-3}$.
This assumption is extremely important for the traditional architecture of \EB{} codes,
because it means that the energy contribution to the Friedmann equation (\enquote{expansion history}) is independent of thermodynamics.
CLASS, for example, has a rigid structure with $\texttt{input.c} \rightarrow \texttt{background.c} \rightarrow \texttt{thermodynamics.c}$ that \emph{requires} such a decoupling of the \enquote{background} from \enquote{thermodynamics} \cite{lesgourguesCosmicLinearAnisotropy2011d}.
This rigid structure is made even harder to disentangle by \EB{} codes facilitating substitution of different \enquote{recombination codes} that do not couple to the background.

However, this structure is incomplete because baryons have a nonzero temperature $T > 0$ and a small but nonzero pressure $P > 0$.
For example, $P = nT$ assuming an ideal gas.
This makes $w_b \neq 0$ a generally time-dependent function that is coupled to thermodynamical variables like $T_b$ and $X_e$,
so a fully consistent treatment should really solve the continuity equation
\begin{equation}
\dot{\rho}_b = -3\calH(1+w_b)\rho_b
\end{equation}
numerically with a \emph{coupled} \enquote{background} and \enquote{thermodynamics}.
It seems to me that a fully consistent implementation is not available anywhere.
Such models are possible to implement in SymBoltz, because the code handles \enquote{background} and \enquote{thermodynamics} as one stage referred to as the background.

The corrections from more consistent treatment has been found to be small \cite{pookkillathBaryonPhysicsTight2019}.
Nevertheless, in my opinion, \EB{} codes should generally handle the \enquote{background} and \enquote{thermodynamics} as one coupled stage to facilitate study of the consistent coupled equations.
It might be necessary to revisit this property for alternative models, particularly if they touch baryon physics in the early universe, where baryons are least relativistic and $w_b$ is highest.
In my opinion, the (de)coupling of the \enquote{background} and \enquote{thermodynamics} should be understood as a model-dependent property in general,
and \enquote{background evolution} should be understood as the evolution of a coupled \enquote{background} and \enquote{thermodynamics}.

\subsection{Recombination models and codes}
\label{sec:modify_recombination}

In the dense and hot early universe photons scatter off charged particles such as electrons and protons.
This is called Compton scattering, or Thomson scattering at low energies $h\nu \ll m c^2$.
The Thomson scattering cross section $\sigma_T = (8\pi/3) \, (\alpha \hbar / m_e c)^2$ depends inversely on mass,
so electrons are most important:
\begin{equation}
e^- + \gamma \longleftrightarrow e^- + \gamma .
\label{eq:thomson}
\end{equation}
The Thomson scattering rate $\dot\kappa = a n_e \sigma_T$ (with respect to conformal time) is proportional to the electron density $n_e$.
\TODO{needed by pert eqs}
It is regulated by combination and ionization of atoms and ions,
predominantly $\Hy$ and $\He$ that make up almost $75\%$ and $25\%$ of the mass of elements in the early universe according to Big Bang Nucleosynthesis:
\begin{equation}
\Hy^+ + e^-    \longleftrightarrow \Hy + \gamma, \quad
\He^+ + e^-    \longleftrightarrow \He + \gamma, \quad
\He^{++} + e^- \longleftrightarrow \He^+ + \gamma.
\label{eq:recombination}
\end{equation}
Recombination codes simulate the rates of these coupled processes
in order to determine the free electron density $n_e$, optical depth $\kappa$ and \enquote{baryon} (electron) temperature $T_b$ as functions of time.
This is very complicated since the rates of each process \eqref{eq:recombination}
depends on the atomic states orbitals $(n, l)$, commonly denoted $1s, 2s, 2p, 3s, 3p, 3d, \ldots$).%
\footnote{In principle $(n,l,m)$, but the magnetic number $-l \leq m \leq +l$ is ignored. Previously $l$ was not resolved, either.}
The radiative transfer equation must also be solved for the mean intensity $J(\tau, \nu)$ of photons as a function of both time and frequency,
which turns the problem from an ODE into a PDE \cite[e.g.][]{seagerHowExactlyDid2000a}.
The problem is further complicated by taking place in an expanding spacetime and out of equilibrium.
In principle, one must evolve a large coupled reaction system for the densities $n_i$ of each state $i \in (n ,l)$ for each atomic species $\Hy, \He, \He^+, \ldots$
\cite[e.g.][]{seagerHowExactlyDid2000a,chlubaCosmologicalHydrogenRecombination2007,grinCosmologicalHydrogenRecombination2010}:
\begin{equation}
a^{-3} \odv{(n_i a^3)}{t} = \big[ n_e n_c P_{ci} - n_i P_{ic} \big] + \sum_j \big[ n_j P_{ji} - n_i P_{ij} \big] ,
\quad i = (n, l) ,
\label{eq:fullrec}
\end{equation}
where $n_c$ is the density of the ionized version of $i$ (e.g. $\Hy^+$ for $\Hy$)
and $P_{ij}$ give reaction rates between states as functions of all current densities and temperatures.
This quickly turns into a slow and challenging computational problem:
the equations are very stiff, and the size of the system scales as $O(n_\text{max}^2)$ and each atomic species needs $n_\text{max} \gtrsim 100$ for accurate results.
Significant research has been devoted to exploring recombination models,
identifying additional effects that must be treated to meet ever-growing precision requirements,
and to find effective models that strike a tighter balance between performance and accuracy and can be solved in milliseconds within \EB{} codes.

\subsubsection{Peebles' equation}

The basic recombination picture was established independently by \cite{peeblesRecombinationPrimevalPlasma1968,zeldovichRecombinationHydrogenHot1968},
and is the one implemented in COSMICS \cite{maCosmologicalPerturbationTheory1995a}.
It is an effective three-state model of Hydrogen:
the ground state 1s ($n=1$),
the first excited states 2s and 2p assumed to be in equilibrium ($n=2$),
and the continuum of ions and electrons ($n=\infty$).
Other excited states $3 \leq n < \infty$ are assumed to be so short-lived that they immediately decay to $n=2$.
Let us consider the three paths between $(1, 2, \infty)$.

Direct transitions between $1 \leftrightarrow \infty$ have no net effect and need not be included in the calculation.
Combination directly to the ground state emits a photon with $E \geq 13.6\,\mathrm{eV}$ that immediately reionizes a nearby atom and cancels the effect.
Peebles' equation therefore only describes the two transitions $1 \leftrightarrow 2 \leftrightarrow \infty$:
\begin{equation}
\dot{x}_\Hy = -a C_r \left[ \alpha^{(2)}(T_b) n_\Hy x_e x_p - \beta(T_\gamma) (1-x_p) \right].
\label{eq:peebles}
\end{equation}
%Everything begins fully ionized with $x_p = 1$ in the hot early universe.
The left term in brackets describes electrons and protons that meet in $n=\infty$ at a rate proportional to $x_e x_p$, and combine to $n=2$ with temperature-dependent rate $\alpha^{(2)}(T_b)$.
The right term similarly describes neutral \Hy{} with density proportional to $1-x_p$ ionizing from $n=2$ to $n=\infty$ with rate $\beta(T_\gamma)$,
which is expressed in terms of $\alpha^{(2)}(T_b)$ by a detailed balance argument.
The prefactor accounts for the fraction of atoms in $n=2$ that decay to $n=1$ before being reionized:
\TODO{correct?}
\begin{equation}
C_r = \frac{\Lambda_{2s\to1s} + \Lambda_{2p\to1s}}{\Lambda_{2s\to1s} + \Lambda_{2p\to1s} + \beta^{(2)}},
\qquad
\Lambda_{2p\to1s} = A_{2p\to1s} \, P_\text{esc}.
\label{eq:peebles_factor}
\end{equation}
This can happen in one of two ways.
First, the decay $2p \to 1s$ is allowed and emits a Lyman-$\alpha$ photon,
but the photon is likely to reexcite $1s \to 2p$ in a nearby atom \emph{unless} it first redshifts to lower energy.
This is modeled by the decay rate $A_{2p\to1s}$ and \enquote{escape} probability $P_\text{esc}$ with the Sobolev approximation,
which depends on $\calH$ and cosmology and avoids solving the radiative transfer equations.
Second, the decay $2s \to 1s$ is forbidden with one emitted photon because it violates the $\Delta l = \pm 1$ selection rule,
but occurs at rate $\Lambda_{2s\to1s}$ through two-photon emission, neither of which can be reabsorbed by nearby atoms.

\subsubsection{RECFAST}

RECFAST \cite{seagerNewCalculationRecombination1999b} was for a long time the standard recombination code.
It refines Peebles' model by first simulating a full and expensive recombination problem \eqref{eq:fullrec} of $\Hy$, $\Hy^+$, $\He$, $\He^+$, $\He^{++}$, $e^-$, $\Hy^-$, $\Hy_2$ and $\Hy_2^+$ with 609 coupled ODEs.
\TODO{reword so it doesnt sound like 609 eqs are solved every time}
It is then compressed into Peebles-like equations \eqref{eq:peebles} for both $\He$ and $\He^+$ in addition to $\Hy$,
but with a recombination coefficient $\alpha_\Hy$ that is fudged by an arbitrary and unphysical \enquote{fudge factor} $F$
that is tuned to bring the results of the compressed model as close as possible to the full simulation.
\begin{equation}
\alpha_\Hy(T_b)
\quad \to \quad 
F\cdot\alpha_\Hy(T_b)
\quad \text{with} \quad
F = 1.14 . \\
\end{equation}
This combines the accuracy of the multi-level computation with the speed of Peebles' equation,
but the fitting assumes a \LCDM{}-like model with particular parameter values.
This makes RECFAST unsafe to use with modified cosmological models.

Since the original version, RECFAST has been updated several times with more fitting functions and fudge factors to account for additional effects that have been found to be important.
For example, non-negligible effects have been found from \He{} recombination taking place through distinct singlet and triplet states,
and by resolving both $(n,l)$ in multi-level simulations (instead of just $n$).
\cite{wongHowWellWe2007,rubino-martinEstimatingImpactRecombination2010,chlubaRadiativeTransferEffects2012a,collaborationPlanck2013Results2014}.
The increasingly unsatisfactory ad-hoc nature of RECFAST motivated more physically principled recombination codes (below).

\subsubsection{CosmoRec, HyRec and HyRec-2}

\begin{figure}[t!]
\centering

\caption{\label{fig:emla}

\enquote{Interface states} are directly connected to the ground state with radiative transitions, and are cheap to solve but cosmology-dependent.
\enquote{Interior states} are other excited states that are only indirectly connected to the ground state, and are expensive to solve but cosmology-independent,

CosmoRec \cite{chlubaCompleteTreatmentCosmological2010} and HyRec \cite{ali-haimoudHyRecFastHighly2011a}
are newer and more physically principled recombination codes that avoid the fudge factors of RECFAST.
They are both based on a new method called the effective multi-level atom (EMLA) \cite{ali-haimoudUltrafastEffectiveMultilevel2010}.
As shown in \cref{fig:emla}, it separates all excited $(n, l)$ states into two categories:
a small set of \enquote{interface states} $i$ that are directly connected to the ground state with radiative transitions,
and complementary \enquote{interior states} that are only indirectly connected to it.

The central result of the EMLA is that instead of evolving ODEs for all states in the full recombination system \eqref{eq:fullrec},
the effect of the \enquote{interior states} can be accounted for \emph{exactly} (under some reasonable assumptions) by effective combination and ionization coefficients $A_i(T_b, T_\gamma)$ and $B_i(T_\gamma)$ to each of the \enquote{interface states}.
These coefficients depend only on the baryon and the photon temperatures and \emph{not} on cosmology,
so they can be precomputed once and for all cosmologies with a many-level computation and tabulated.
The problem then reduces to tracking only a small set of interface states and interpolating $A_i$ and $B_i$ in one or two variables,
and the remaining set of equations for the interface states that must be solved per cosmology are small:
\begin{equation}
\dot{x}_e = -\sum_i \big( n_\Hy x_e^2 A_i(T_b, T_\gamma) - x_i B_i(T_\gamma) \big), \qquad i \in \{2s, 2p, \ldots\} .
\label{eq:hyrec}
\end{equation}

While HyRec and CosmoRec both use the very efficient EMLA,
they differ mostly in how they solve the radiation transfer problem.
The codes treat this by solving for the full photon intensity distribution $J(\tau, \nu)$
instead of using the Peebles factor \eqref{eq:peebles_factor} with the Sobolev approximation.
This remains the performance bottleneck in the problem, and makes HyRec and CosmoRec slower than RECFAST.
HyRec-2 alleviates this problem by fitting a function $\Delta$ around the Sobolev approximation
\begin{equation}
R_{2p\to1s} = \frac{R_\mathrm{Ly\alpha}}{1+\Delta} .
\end{equation}
Here $\Delta(\tau; \omega_b, \omega_c, N_\mathrm{eff})$ is a first-order Taylor expansion through the Planck 2018 best-fit parameters
that makes $\dot{x}_e$ match exactly for those particular parameters.
This is a much less severe form of the fudge factors in RECFAST and sacrifices some generality,
but at the end of the day it makes HyRec-2 both faster and more accurate than RECFAST.

Using the original RECFAST over HyRec and CosmoRec can give $1\%$-$3\%$-$4\%$ error in $x_e$ and up to $4\%$ error in $C_l$ at high $l \approx 3000$,
which in turn noticeably biases extracted parameters from Planck \cite{shawPreciseCosmologicalParameter2011}.

but the fit to a \LCDM{} remains less safe for extended models than with HyRec and CosmoRec.
HyRec-2 is now the standard recombination code in CLASS,
while CAMB can optionally be compiled for it but still defaults to RECFAST recalibrated against HyRec and CosmoRec.
SymBoltz also offers both RECFAST and HyRec-2.

\subsection{Variation of fundamental constants}

Fundamental physical constants like Newton's constant $G$, the electron mass $m_e$ and the fine-structure constant $\alpha$ are measured to high precision today,
but a class of extended models have replaced them by corrections that depend on time and sometimes space.
%there is an open possibility that they (or their effective values) have evolved over time.

A changing electron mass $m_e$ fine-structure constant $\alpha$ modifies recombination.
%It affects the Thomson scattering rate $\sigma_T = (8\pi/3) \alpha^2 / m_e^2$ \cite{greeneThomsonScatteringOne2023}
Increasing $\alpha$ or $m_e$ increases the Hydrogen ionization energy $E_\mathrm{ion} = \alpha^2 e c^2 / 2$,
which moves recombination to higher temperature at an earlier time,
so the sound horizon at decoupling is shorter
and the acoustic peaks of the CMB power spectrum is moved to smaller scales at higher $\ell$
\cite{kaplinghatConstrainingVariationsFineStructure1999,hartNewConstraintsTimedependent2018}.
This is a viable route to alleviating the $H_0$ tension, for example \cite{sekiguchiEarlyRecombinationSolution2021,schonebergH0OlympicsFair2022}.
%Thomson scattering $\sigma_T \propto \alpha^2 / m_e^2$ \TODO{?}

A changing gravitational constant $G$ affects the expansion history through the Friedmann equation $H^2 = 8\pi G \rho / 3$,
and the perturbations through the Poisson equation $\nabla^2 \psi = 4 \pi G a^2 \delta \rho$.
For example, a time-independent increase of $G$ leads to increased damping of the CMB power spectrum at high $\ell$ \cite{umezuCosmologicalConstraintsNewtons2005,galliCavendishPLANCKConstraining2009,lamineCosmologicalMeasurementGravitational2025}.
Several modified gravity theories also lead to effective changes in $G$,
including \BD{} gravity in particular \TODO{ref somewhere}.

\subsubsection{Phenomenologically based}

MGCAMB \cite{zhaoSearchingModifiedGrowth2009} and
MGCLASS \cite{bakerObservationalSignaturesModified2015,sakrCosmologicalConstraintsSubhorizon2022}
take a phenomenological approach to modified gravity.
They modify the Einstein equations for the metric potentials $\phi$ and $\psi$ in GR (where $\mu=\eta=1$ below)
with a freely chosen \enquote{gravitational coupling} $\mu(a,k)$ and \enquote{slip} $\eta(a, k)$:
\begin{subequations}
\begin{align}
k^2\psi &= -4\pi \, \mu(a,k) \, a^2 \big( \rho \Delta + 3(\rho+P)\sigma \big), \label{eq:mg_poisson} \\
k^2\big(\phi - \gamma(a,k) \psi\big) &= 12\pi \mu(a,k) \, a^2 (\rho+P)\sigma \label{eq:mg_slip} .
\end{align}%
\label{eq:mg}%
\end{subequations}
Without shear stress $\sigma = 0$,
this becomes $k^2 \psi = -4\pi\mu a^2 \rho \Delta$ and $\phi = \gamma \psi$,
which are common approximations in $N$-body simulations in the late universe, for example.
But in \EB{} codes it is necessary to account for the shear stress of photons and neutrinos in the early universe.

Equations \eqref{eq:mg} are not plug-and-play replacements in \EB{} codes.
The continuity equations \eqref{eq:pert_cont} require $\dot\phi$,
which is directly available in standard \LCDM{} implementations that evolve a differential equation for $\dot\phi$.
Modified gravity codes must instead obtain $\dot\phi$ by differentiating equations \eqref{eq:mg},
which results in long expressions \cite[e.g.][in the synchronous gauge]{zuccaMGCAMBMassiveNeutrinos2019}.
Similarly, the line-of-sight integral for the CMB source function \TODO{ref} needs $\dot\psi$.
This can be computed either by expanding the derivative or by evaluating the derivative numerically with splines after integrating the perturbations.

\subsubsection{Theoretically based}

$\mathcal{H}$-EFTCAMB \cite{huEffectiveFieldTheory2014,raveriEffectiveFieldTheory2014} \TODO{cite \url{https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.01662}} and
hi\_class \cite{zumalacarreguiHiclassHorndeskiCosmic2017a,belliniHiclassBackgroundEvolution2020a}
implement theoretically principled models of modified gravity and effective field theory (EFT).
This is generally a very large space of models.

In particular, both codes support Horndeski theories of gravity.
This is the most general class of theories with second order equations of motion
from a metric tensor $g_{\mu\nu}$ and scalar field $\varphi$ as degrees of freedom.
Any Horndeski theory is specified by four free functions
of $\varphi$ and the kinetic term $X = -(\partial_\mu \varphi)^2/2$:
\begin{equation}
G_2(\varphi, X), \quad G_3(\varphi, X), \quad G_4(\varphi, X), \quad G_5(\varphi, X) .
\label{eq:horndeski}
\end{equation}

The background gravity equations are schematically modified as \cite[e.g.][]{belliniHiclassBackgroundEvolution2020a}:
\begin{subequations}
\begin{align}
0 &= C(H) = C_3 H^3 + C_2 H^2 + C_1 H + C_0 , \label{eq:hifried1} \\
\dot{H} &= \dot{H}(H, \varphi, \dot{\varphi}), \label{eq:hifried2} \\
\ddot{\varphi} &= \ddot{\varphi}(H, \varphi, \dot{\varphi}) . \label{eq:hikg}
\end{align}%
\label{eq:hibg}%
\end{subequations}
The 1st Friedmann equation \eqref{eq:hifried1} turns into a cubic constraint equation for the Hubble function $H(\tau)$,
which is impractical to solve explicitly for $H$.
The 2nd Friedmann (acceleration) equation \eqref{eq:hifried2} remains a first order ODE for $H(\tau)$ (or second order for $a(\tau)$).
The \KG{} equation \eqref{eq:hikg} is a new second order ODE for the scalar field.
These equations are solved differently than in \LCDM{},
where the constraint \eqref{eq:fried1} has the form $C_2 H^2 + C_0 = 0$ and is usually integrated directly as $H = +\sqrt{-C_0/C_2}$ without using the acceleration equation.
Instead, equations \eqref{eq:hifried2} and \eqref{eq:hikg} are integrated as ODEs, but with a few nuances:
\begin{itemize}
\item The initial condition for $H(\tau_\ini)$ is found by solving the constraint equation \eqref{eq:hifried1} with a nonlinear root solver (e.g. a Newton method).
Initial conditions for $\varphi(\tau_\ini)$ and $\dot{\varphi}(\tau_\ini)$ are set freely or with predetermined expressions on a model-by-model basis.
\item The constraint \eqref{eq:hifried1} is accounted for over time by constraint dampening:
\begin{equation}
\dot{H} \to \dot{H} - a \gamma_H \cdot \sign(C^\prime(H)) \cdot C(H), \quad (a \gamma_H > 0).
\end{equation}
An extra term proportional to the violation of the constraint $C(H)$ is added to the ODE for $H$.
It is unchanged when $C(H) = 0$; but when $C(H) \neq 0$ is violated, the ODE \enquote{pushes} $H$ back in the direction where the constraint is better satisfied.
This technique is necessary because integrating \eqref{eq:hifried2} alone pays no attention to the constraint,
so deviations from it grow over time.
It is common with overdetermined systems that have more equations than variables,
such as the Einstein equations, where a subset of equations must be chosen to integrate with numerical methods.
\item One parameter or initial condition must usually be found with the shooting method,
as not all choices of $H_0$ (treated as a free parameter), $\varphi(\tau_\ini)$ and $\dot{\varphi}(\tau_\ini)$ integrate such that $H(\tau_0)$ takes the value $H_0$.
\end{itemize}

The gravitational perturbation equations are also modified.
They can be arranged schematically as four redundant Einstein equations
and one perturbed \KG{} equation for the metric potentials $\phi$ and $\psi$
and the scalar field perturbation $v_X = -a\,\delta\varphi / \dot{\varphi}$
\cite{belliniMaximalFreedomMinimum2014a}:
\begin{subequations}
\begin{align}
A_1 \dot{\phi} + A_2 \dot{v}_X + A_3 \phi + A_4 \psi + A_5 v_X &= A, && (G_{00}) \label{eq:hipt1} \\
B_1 \dot{\phi} + B_2 \dot{v}_X + B_3 \psi + B_4 v_X &= B, && (G_{0i}) \label{eq:hipt2} \\
C_1 \phi + C_2 \psi + C_3 v_X &= C, && (G_{ij}) \label{eq:hipt3} \\
D_1 \ddot{\phi} + D_2 \ddot{v}_X + D_3 \dot{\phi} + D_4 \dot{\psi} + D_5 \dot{v}_X + D_6 \psi + D_7 v_X &= D, && (G_{ii}) \label{eq:hipt4} \\
E_1 \ddot{\phi} + E_2 \ddot{v}_X + E_3 \dot{\phi} + E_4 \dot{\psi} + E_5 \dot{v}_X + E_6 \phi + E_7 \psi + E_8 v_X &= 0 , && (\delta\varphi) \label{eq:hipt5}
\end{align}
\end{subequations}
where the right sides $A$-$D$ are energy-momentum contributions from matter,
and the left side coefficients $A_1$-$E_8$ depend on the background,
in particular through four time-dependent functions $\alpha_K$, $\alpha_B$, $\alpha_T$ and $\alpha_M$
that are uniquely determined from $G_i$ evaluated on the background.
Some coefficients also have factors of $k^2$.

These overdetermined equations can be solved in the following way:
a second order ODE for $\ddot{v}_X$ by eliminating $\ddot{\phi}$ from \eqref{eq:hipt4} and \eqref{eq:hipt5};
a first order ODE for $\dot\phi$ from either \eqref{eq:hipt1} or \eqref{eq:hipt2};
and an algebraic equation for $\psi$ from \eqref{eq:hipt3}.
This also needs $\dot{\psi}$, which can be found by differentiating the $\psi$ expression once.
This is similar in principle to \LCDM{} apart from the addition of the scalar field equation,
but much more convoluted as the coefficients have quite long expressions.

In particular, the modifications introduce additional stiffness into the perturbation ODEs,
and hi\_class introduces its own quasi-static approximation scheme (QSA) on top of several other approximation schemes in CLASS
for evolving the $\ddot{v}_X$ ODE \cite[][section 5]{belliniHiclassBackgroundEvolution2020a}:
\begin{displayquote}
Models with large mass have very high oscillation frequencies, and consequently Eq. (5.1) has to be integrated with very small step sizes.
This can cause the code to slow down and potentially to crash when the integration step size becomes smaller than machine precision.
[\ldots]
The idea of the QS approximation is that there is a degree of freedom which can react on a timescale much faster than any other in the system.
\end{displayquote}

Although the word is not used in the hi\_class papers, this is a manifestation of stiffness in the equations
where different time scales simultaneously appear in the equations and make them challenging to solve numerically.
Instead of complicating the equations with approximation schemes, it can be handled with implicit ODE solvers (see \cref{sec:approx}).

Several \EB{} codes for modified gravity have been compared and found to produce errors no larger than $1\%$ on observables for a range of popular theories,
such as \BD{} gravity, $f(R)$ gravity and covariant Galileons (each of which corresponds to particular $G_i$) \cite{belliniComparisonEinsteinBoltzmannSolvers2018a}.

\subsection{Interacting models}
\label{sec:modify_interacting}

All species in the standard \LCDM{} model indirectly with each other through gravity,
but only baryons and photons interact directly through Thomson scattering.
Total energy-momentum conservation $\nabla_\mu T^{\mu\nu} = 0$ is satisfied
by separately conserving $\nabla_\mu T^{\mu\nu}_a = 0$ for noninteracting species.
Interacting models lift this assumption and allows for a nonzero flow $Q_{ab}$ between pairs $(a,b)$ of species:
\begin{equation}
\nabla_\mu T^{\mu\nu}_{a} = Q_a = \sum_b Q_{ab}
\quad \text{with} \quad
Q_{ab} = -Q_{ba} .
\end{equation}
The antisymmetric flow ensures that the total is conserved: $\sum_s \nabla_\mu T^{\mu\nu}_s = \sum_{a,b} Q_{ab} = -\sum_{a,b} Q_{ab} = 0$.
\Cref{tab:interactions} shows examples of such interactions that have been implemented in modified \EB{} codes.

Such models lead to modification of the background (including thermodynamics) and perturbation equations.
For example, it may modify the continuity equations in the background to
\begin{equation}
\dot{\rho}_a = -3\calH\,(1+w_a)\rho_a + Q_a.
\end{equation}
They no longer have analytical solutions $\rho \propto a^{-3(1+w_a)}$ with constant $w_a$ for arbitrary $Q_a$,
so they must instead integrated numerically.
If the model is to parameterized in the conventional way by their present densities $\Omega_{a0}$,
this makes it necessary to either integrate the background backwards in time (from $a=1$ to $a \ll 1$)
or use the shooting method to determine the initial conditions that give the desired parameters.

The perturbation equations must also be modified \cite[e.g.][appendix A]{figuerueloDarkMatterbaryonsElastic2026}:
\begin{subequations}
\begin{align}
\delta_b &= -\theta_b + 3 \dot{\phi}, \\
\theta_b &= -\calH \theta_c + k^2 \psi + c_s^2 k^2 \delta_b + \dot{\kappa} \frac{4 \rho_\gamma}{3 \rho_b} (\theta_\gamma - \theta_b) + \frac{\gamma}{a^2} (\rho_c - \rho_b) , \\
\delta_c &= -\theta_c + 3 \dot{\phi}, \\
\theta_b &= -\calH \delta_c + k^2 \psi - \frac{\gamma}{a^2} \frac{\rho_b}{\rho_c} (\theta_c - \theta_b) .
\end{align}
\end{subequations}
Such modifications frequently touch the approximation schemes in \EB{} codes.
This makes it necessary to modify the TCA \cite[e.g.][]{wilkinsonUsingCMBAngular2014,chenCosmicMicrowaveBackground2002}
or to derive new TCAs \cite[e.g.][]{archidiaconoConstrainingDarkMatter2019}.

These factors complicate the implementation of interacting models.

The implementation of massive neutrinos in \EB{} codes is complicated by their generally nonzero mass.
It is necessary to solve for perturbations $\Psi$ to their phase-space distribution function (in Fourier space)
$f(\tau, k, q) = f_0(q) (1 + \Psi(\tau, k, q, \mu))$
as a function of their momenta $q$ and direction $\mu = \vec{k} \cdot \vec{q}$ with the Fourier wavenumber $k$.
This leads to a differential equation:
\begin{equation}
\pdv{\Psi}{\tau} + i k \mu \frac{q}{\epsilon} \Psi + \odv{\log f_0}{\log q} \left[ \dot{\phi} - i k \mu \frac{\epsilon}{q} \psi \right] = 0 . %\frac{1}{f_0} \pdv{f}{\tau}
\end{equation}
The RHS becomes nonzero in the presence of interactions.

% Route A
% energy densities etc are given by an integral over q (over both q and angles)
The standard way to tackle this problem is to expand $\Psi$ in multipoles:
\begin{equation}
\Psi = \sum_{l=0}^\infty (-1)^l (2l+1) \Psi_l(\tau,k,q) P_l(\mu) .
\end{equation}
This results in a hierarchy of equations for each multipole:
\begin{align}
\dot{\Psi}_0 &= -\frac{qk}{\epsilon} \Psi_1 - \dot{\phi}\,\odv{\log f_0}{\log q} , \\
\dot{\Psi}_1 &= \frac{qk}{3\epsilon} (\Psi_0 - 2\Psi_2) - \frac{\epsilon k}{3q} \psi \odv{\log f_0}{\log q} , \\
\dot{\Psi}_l &= \frac{qk}{(2l+1)\epsilon} (l \Psi_{l-1} - (l+1) \Psi_{l+1}) ,
\label{eq:neutrino_hierarchy}
\end{align}
with some cutoff.
The multipole expansion
\begin{align}
\delta\rho &= 4 \pi a^{-4} \int \diff{q}\,q^2 \epsilon f_0(q) \Psi_0 , \\
\ldots
\label{eq:neutrino_quadrature}
\end{align}
It is not possible to integrate out the dependence on the momentum $q$.
This means that the integrals \eqref{eq:neutrino_quadrature} must be approximated by numerical quadrature schemes $I[g(q)] = \int \diff{q}\,f_0(q) g(q) \approx \sum_i W_i g(q_i)$,
and that the hierarchy \eqref{eq:neutrino_hierarchy} must be evolved on a grid of both $l$ and $q$.

This makes massive neutrinos very computationally intensive.

Some modifications of \EB{} codes have dealt with finding more efficient ways to deal with the momentum bins and hierarchy.
COSMICS tackled the integral with 128 $q$-points per $k$-mode \cite{maCosmologicalPerturbationTheory1995a}, which is unnecessarily many.
CAMB grew out of COSMICS and followed the even sampling \cite{lesgourguesCosmicLinearAnisotropy2011f},
CLASS uses Gaussian quadrature, which samples nonuniformly in $q$ for a far more efficient quadrature rule,
and gets away with only 5 $q$-bins \cite{lesgourguesCosmicLinearAnisotropy2011f} while maintaining good accuracy.
This prompted CAMB to switch to a similar quadrature rule with only 3/4/5-points \cite[appendix A]{howlettCMBPowerSpectrum2012a}.
CLASS also includes fluid approximations that switches to a $(\dot\delta, \dot\theta, \dot\sigma)$ system instead of the hierarchy \eqref{eq:neutrino_hierarchy} \cite{lesgourguesCosmicLinearAnisotropy2011f},
which cuts the number of ODEs to 3.
CAMB also features an expansion in $am/q \ll 1$ that avoids evolving separate $q$ at early times and for high $q$,
and also lowers $l$ as neutrinos become nonrelativistic in the late universe \cite{lewisEvolutionCosmologicalDark2002,howlettCMBPowerSpectrum2012a}.

\end{equation}
and then expand in multipoles:
\begin{align}
\Psi_l(\tau) &= \sum_{l'=0}^\infty (2l'+1) W_{l l'}(k \chi_q(\tau_i,\tau)) \Psi_{l'}(\tau_i) \nonumber \\
             &+ \int_{\tau_i}^\tau \odv{\log f_0}{\log q} \diff\tau \left[ -j_l(k\chi_q(\tau',\tau_f)) \dot{\eta} - j_l^{\prime\prime}(k\chi_q(\tau',\tau_f))\frac{\dot{h}+6\dot{\eta}}{2} \right]

Iterates each $k$-mode.

\section{The development and features of SymBoltz}
\label{sec:symboltz}

Throughout my PhD I became increasingly interested in \EB{} codes.
First, I learned to use CLASS myself while working on \cref{paper:bransdicke} in this thesis.
Second, it is a mandatory project\footnote{\url{https://cmb.wintherscoming.no/}} in the \enquote{Cosmology 2} course at UiO to write a simplified \EB{} code
that solves the background, recombination with the Peebles equation, perturbations, and computes matter and CMB power spectra.
Third, through my time as a PhD student I have seen countless papers that implement extended cosmological models in \EB{} codes (see \cref{sec:modifications}),
and become familiar with how it can be difficult to navigate and understand the source code of these codes,
and how the complicated parts of \EB{} codes are sometimes treated as \enquote{black boxes}.

It was the sum of these pieces gave me insight into how \EB{} codes work,
and inspired me to write a new code from scratch.
It is designed to be even easier to modify than existing codes through a symbolic-numeric interface;
avoid approximation schemes found in CAMB and CLASS in order to simplify the internals and make enable users to write down a \emph{single} set of equations for their model;
and support modern numerical techniques such as automatic differentiation and improved interpolation methods.
In this section, I will explain this design in greater detail.

\subsection{Symbolic-numeric interface for easy model specification}
\label{sec:symbolicnumeric}

As \cref{sec:modifications} shows, the standard \LCDM{} \EB{} equations \eqref{eq:eb} are highly subject to modification.
To modify the equations in other \EB{} codes,
one must often make significant edits to the source code in many different places.
For example, in CLASS one must generally edit some or all of the following files:
\begin{itemize}
\item \texttt{input.c} and \texttt{input.h} for parameter handling,
\item \texttt{background.c} and \texttt{background.h}: background equations,
\item \texttt{thermodynamics.c} and \texttt{thermodynamics.h} for thermodynamics equations,
\item \texttt{perturbations.c} and \texttt{perturbations.h} for perturbation equations.
\end{itemize}
Moreover, each file must typically be edited in several places:
initial conditions, evolution equations, approximation schemes, parameter handling and input/output handling are handled in different places in each file.
Navigating and understanding all these files is a nontrivial task for users that are unfamiliar with the code.
At the time of writing these files contain more than 20 000 lines of code in total,
where the high-level equations are intertwined with low-level machinery.
In my opinion, this separation of the modules in CLASS reflect the right way to \emph{solve} the \EB{} problem,
but make for an unnecessarily complicated way to \emph{specify} it.

With SymBoltz, my vision is to separate out the full specification of the \EB{} equations \eqref{eq:eb}
from internal machinery in the code.
In particular, I advocate for thinking about the full \EB{} system of equations \eqref{eq:eb}
-- which includes what is commonly called the \enquote{background}, \enquote{thermodynamics}, \enquote{recombination code} and \enquote{perturbations} --
as fundamentally \emph{one} (large) system of ODEs that should also be specified as such (including all differential and algebraic equations, parameter definitions and initial conditions).
For example, the perturbation and background equations should be specified in the \emph{same} system.
It should be the responsibility of the cosmologist (\enquote{user}) to specify this large system of equations for their model;
and it should be the responsibility of the code to transform the system into an optimal numerical problem and to solve it,
such as breaking up the equations into \enquote{background}, \enquote{thermodynamics} and \enquote{perturbation} stages.
Provided that this symbolic framework is sufficiently general,
it provides a common interface to modify any part of the \EB{} equations in a way that accommodates any well-defined extension of the equations.

The easiest way to see how the equations are specified in SymBoltz is to look at one of the examples\footnote{\url{https://hersle.github.io/SymBoltz.jl/stable/LCDM/}} in the online documentation of SymBoltz.
It specifies the full \EB{} system \eqref{eq:eb} in around 300 lines of code,
with the background thermodynamics, RECFAST recombination and perturbations all in the same place.

This is the core idea with the mixed symbolic-numeric interface in SymBoltz.
It relies on ModelingToolkit.jl\footnote{\url{https://github.com/SciML/ModelingToolkit.jl/}},
which is a Julia library designed for mixed symbolic-numeric computations.
Significant work in SymBoltz has been contributed back to this library.
\Cref{paper:symboltz} describes how it works and what it achieves.

The background ODEs \eqref{eq:bg_ode} and, in particular, the perturbation ODEs \eqref{eq:pt_ode} are \emph{stiff} \cite{nadkarni-ghoshEinsteinBoltzmannEquationsRevisited2017a}.
This is a property of differential equations that may arise if several timescales coexist in the equations,
and is very common in cosmology where the universe expands on a slow timescale while the photons and baryons interact on a very fast timescale, for example.
Understanding this property is crucial to numerically solving the \EB{} equations,
and there are two different ways of handling it.

\subsubsection{Explicit integrators with approximation schemes}

\begin{figure}[ht!]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.7\textwidth]{media/approximations}
\caption{%
Validity regions in conformal time $\tau$ and perturbation wavenumber $k$ of the
the tight coupling approximation (TCA),
radiation streaming approximation (RSA) and
ultra-relativistic fluid approximation (UFA)
in CLASS for some choice of cosmological parameters.
Reproduced from \cite{blasCosmicLinearAnisotropy2011c}.}
\label{fig:approximations}
\end{figure}

Standard explicit ODE methods, such as the well-known Euler, RK4 and Dormand--Prince methods, fail catastrophically at solving stiff ODEs.
The stiffness causes them to take tiny time steps and diverge with enormous errors.
CAMB and CLASS remove the stiffness in the equations by approximating them with different versions that are valid in different regimes.
Several approximation schemes have been invented, including the
tight coupling approximation (TCA),
radiation streaming approximation (RSA),
ultra-relativistic fluid approximation (UFA) and
non-cold dark matter fluid approximation (NCDMFA) \cite{blasCosmicLinearAnisotropy2011c,lesgourguesCosmicLinearAnisotropy2011f}.
All of them involve switching between different variations of the \EB{} system \eqref{eq:eb} at different $\tau$ and $k$.
The first three are illustrated by \cref{sec:approx}.

For example, the TCA rewrites the baryon and photon equations for $\dot\theta_b$ and $\dot\theta_\gamma=3kF_1/4$
in the \EB{} equations \eqref{eq:eb} into a modified pair:
\begin{subequations}
\begin{align}
\dot\theta_b &= -\frac{1}{1+R}\left(\calH\theta_b - c_s^2 k^2 \delta_b - k^2 R\left(\tfrac{1}{4}\delta_\gamma - \sigma_\gamma^{\mathrm{tca}}\right) + R\,\dot\Theta_{\gamma b}^{\mathrm{tca}}\right) + k^2\psi, \\
\dot\theta_\gamma &= \dot\theta_b + \dot\Theta_{\gamma b}^{\mathrm{tca}},
\end{align}
\end{subequations}
where $\sigma_\gamma^\mathrm{tca}$ and $\dot\Theta_{\gamma b}^\mathrm{tca}$
are perturbative power series in the smallness parameter $\tau = 1/\dot\kappa$
that measures the (short) mean free path of photons in the rapidly interacting photon-baryon plasma in the early universe
\cite{blasCosmicLinearAnisotropy2011c}.
This expansion is done to both first and second order, and in either case yields complicated expressions.
Moreover, one must identify the $(\tau, k)$-regime where the approximation is valid,
and tune a hyperparameter that decides when to switch the approximation scheme on and off.
Finally, one should also validate any approximation scheme against the non-approximate equations,
which call for a way of deactivating them anyway.

Suppose that one goes through all the trouble of correctly deriving, implementing, tuning and validating
all the necessary approximation schemes for the \LCDM{} model, and leans back to relax.
In the next moment, someone enters the room and wants to implement an extended model
in the \EB{} code that requires modifications of the equations.
It is not unlikely that the modifications will touch one or more of the approximation schemes
and require the whole process to be repeated for the modified equations.
Strictly speaking, this is true even for the modifications that do not touch the approximated equations directly,
because the tuning and validation in principle depends on the whole coupled perturbation ODE.
For example, hi\_class requires the quasi-static approximation (QSA),
and interacting models may require modification of the TCA (see \cref{sec:modifications}).

The use of approximation schemes make it possible to use explicit ODE solvers,
but at the cost of significantly complicating the modeling for users.
They also reduce the size of the ODEs,
as several approximation schemes effectively truncate the hierarchies \eqref{eq:hierarchy_l} and \eqref{eq:hierarchy_ql} in $l$ and $q$
to effective fluid treatments \eqref{eq:fluid}.
Together, they help both speed and stability of the equations,
but they are not the only way to handle stiffness.

\subsubsection{Implicit integrators without approximation schemes}

SymBoltz and PyCosmo handle stiffness in a different way,
by instead embracing it and solving the full stiff equations with implicit ODE solvers.
This completely avoids approximation schemes and lets users specify \emph{one} \EB{} system to evolve at all times.
However, it comes with some additional challenges that are related to the numerics of the code instead of the physics of the equations.

Implicit ODE solvers are designed precisely to handle stiffness.
A pedagogical way of introducing them is to contrast the explicit and implicit Euler methods:%
\footnote{These are \emph{not} the methods used in SymBoltz or any other \EB{} code,
but they offer a simple way to explain implicit ODE methods without complicating the high-level picture with multiple stages and coefficients.}
\begin{subequations}
\begin{align}
u_{n+1} &= u_n + \Delta\tau \cdot f(u_{n  }) \label{eq:euler_explicit}, \\
u_{n+1} &= u_n + \Delta\tau \cdot f(u_{n+1}) \label{eq:euler_implicit}.
\end{align}
\end{subequations}
The explicit method \eqref{eq:euler_explicit} finds the ODE state $u_{n+1}$ at the next time step as a straightforward explicit expression in terms of the current state $u_{n}$ only.
The implicit method \eqref{eq:euler_implicit} instead expresses an implicit system of equations that must be solved for $u_{n+1}$.
Many implicit ODE solvers,
including \texttt{ndf15} in CLASS, \texttt{Kvaerno5} in DISCO-EB and \texttt{KenCarp4} in Bolt in \cref{tab:comparison},
set up this as a nonlinear equation for $u_{n+1}$ and solve it with a Newton method:
\begin{equation}
F(u_{n+1}) = u_{n+1} - \Delta\tau \cdot f(u_{n+1},\tau_{n+1}) - u_n .
\end{equation}
This requires $F$'s Jacobian matrix $W = \nabla F = I - \Delta\tau \cdot J$ and in turn $f$'s Jacobian $J = \nabla f$.
Iterations with Newton's method are expensive operations,
particularly if the ODE includes many variables.

\textbf{Rosenbrock methods} are a different type of implicit solvers that avoid Newton iterations.
They linearize the ODE function around the current time step:
\begin{equation}
f(u_{n+1}) \approx f(u_{n+1}) + J(u_{n+1}) \cdot (u_{n+1}-u_n) .
\label{eq:rosenbrock_linearize}
\end{equation}
Inserting it in the implicit method \eqref{eq:euler_implicit}
gives a \emph{linear} matrix equation for $u_{n+1}$,
which can be solved much faster and without Newton iterations:
\begin{equation}
\big( 1 - \Delta\tau \, J(u_n) \, \big) u_{n+1} = \big( 1 + \Delta\tau \, J(u_n) \big) u_n + \Delta\tau \, f(u_n) .
\label{eq:rosenbrock_linsolve}
\end{equation}

The linearization \eqref{eq:rosenbrock_linearize} is exact only if the ODE is linear with $f = J u$, which is always true for the perturbations \eqref{eq:pt_ode}.
However, Rosenbrock methods also work for nonlinear ODEs such as the background \eqref{eq:bg_ode}.
Higher-order Rosenbrock methods have multiple stages whose coefficients are carefully designed whose coefficients to cancel the linearization error up to some desired order.
For example, the \texttt{Rodas5P} method that SymBoltz uses by default is a 5th order Rosenbrock method that is both stable, accurate and efficient \cite{steinebachConstructionRosenbrockWanner2023}.

A challenge with implicit methods is that the Jacobian $J$ must be evaluated in addition to $f$.
For example, in CLASS the source code is used to specify $f$ through all the derivatives in the \EB{} system \eqref{eq:eb},
and the Jacobian $J$ is found by evaluating $f(u)$ several times with finite differences.
This is sensitive to the finite difference step and not always reliable, and is also unnecessarily inefficient.
\emph{This is where the symbolic-numeric interfaces of SymBoltz and PyCosmo come in.}
With no extra input from the user, the symbolic framework automatically derives the Jacobian $J$ \emph{analytically} from the user-specified equations.
In my opinion, this point is critical to understand, because it ties the ability to avoid approximations down to the fundamental architecture of the \EB{} code.

Finally, the Jacobian of the perturbation ODEs is typically a very sparse matrix.
It is very inefficient to solve the linear system \eqref{eq:rosenbrock_linsolve} with dense methods (that handle all $0$'s explicitly).
Here the symbolic framework again comes in handy, as the Jacobian is precomputed analytically,
so the sparsity pattern is determined ahead of time and the nonzero entries are computed directly by an automatically generated function.
In contrast, a purely numerical code such as CLASS does not \enquote{know} where the zeros are and must determine them at runtime,
which is more expensive than necessary.

\subsection{Gradients through automatic differentiation}

Evaluating derivatives through \EB{} codes is necessary or desirable for Fisher forecasting, gradient-based MCMC methods and for training emulators.
\TODO{ref to intro?}
This is traditionally performed with finite differences,
but automatic differentiation is a more robust way of doing it,
as I explain below.

Any computer program, including an \EB{} code,
can be interpreted as a mathematical function $F: \mathbb{R}^m \rightarrow \mathbb{R}^n$
that takes $m$ input parameters and computes $n$ output numbers.
No matter how complicated the program is, it can always be reduced to a composite function
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:program}
F = f_N \circ f_{N-1} \circ \cdots \circ f_2 \circ f_1,
\qquad \text{i.e.} \qquad
F(p) = f_N(f_{N-1}(\cdots f_2(f_1(p)))),
\end{equation}
of elementary functions $f_i: \mathbb{R}^{m_i} \rightarrow \mathbb{R}^{n_i}$, such as additions, multiplications, exponentials, trigonometric functions and if statements.
I find it intuitive to think of $f_i$ as the $i$-th line of the source code.
For example, an \EB{} code takes in cosmological parameters $p = \{h, \Omega_{b0}, \Omega_{c0}, \ldots\}$ and computes distances, matter power spectra and CMB power spectra $F = \{D(z), P(k), C_\ell, \ldots\}$ at some desired redshifts $z$, wavenumbers $k$ and multipoles $\ell$.

\subsubsection{Finite differences}

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=1.0\textwidth]{media/ad_error}
\caption{%
Error of one-sided and two-sided finite difference derivatives of the matter power spectrum $\pdv{P(k; \mathbf{p})}/{p_i}$ as a function of the step size $\epsilon$ for each cosmological parameter $p_i$ in the legend.
The error is computed relative to the corresponding gradient with automatic differentiation.
%$p = h, \Omega_{c0}, \Omega_{b0}, N_\mathrm{ur}, M_\nu, Y_\mathrm{He}, T_{\gamma 0}, w_0, w_a, ns, \log(10^{10} A_s)$
computed relative to automatic differentiation.}
\label{fig:fd}
\end{figure}

Finite differences is the easiest way of computing derivatives of an arbitrary program.
It runs the program several times for nearby input values
and approximates the limit definition of the derivative,
for example with central finite differences:
\begin{equation}
\pdv{F}{p_i} \approx \frac{F(p_1, \ldots, p_i+\epsilon, \ldots, p_m) - F(p_1, \ldots, p_i-\epsilon, \ldots, p_m)}{2 \epsilon} .
\label{eq:fd}
\end{equation}
This is simple and works on any program by treating it as a \enquote{black box},
but it is approximate and uses $2m$ evaluations of $F$ to compute the gradient with respect to $m$ parameters.
Moreover, it is nontrivial to tune the step $\epsilon$,
as the finite difference approximation itself is more accurate with smaller $\epsilon$,
but too small $\epsilon$ results in numerical round-off errors from noise in $F$.
The best $\epsilon$ generally also differs for each parameter.
This is illustrated in \cref{fig:fd} and is particularly true in \EB{} codes,
which produce numerical noise around the level of internal precision parameters.

\subsubsection{Automatic differentiation}

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{media/ad_runtime}
\caption{%
The performance of automatic differentiation compared to one-sided and two-sided finite differences
for computing the gradient of the matter power spectrum $P(k)$ with respect to the first $N$ parameters in \cref{fig:fd} in SymBoltz version 2.0.
}
\label{fig:ad_performance}
\end{figure}

Automatic differentiation avoids these issues by \enquote{opening the black box}.
Instead, it numerically evaluates the chain rule of the program \eqref{eq:program}:
\begin{equation}
\pdv{F}{p} = J_N \cdot J_{N-1} \cdots J_2 \cdot J_1 .
\label{eq:ad}
\end{equation}
The Jacobian $J_i = \pdv{f_i}/{f_{i-1}}$ is propagated through every elementary function $f_i$ of the program.
\enquote{Automatic} means that the code for $F$ is automatically translated into different code for evaluating $J_i$ without extra user input.
This requires a flexible programming language or compiler, such as Julia or JAX in Python.
The method is numerically exact with no additional hyperparameters to tune
(but still depends on other precision parameters in the code, of course).

While the program \eqref{eq:program} must evaluated inside-to-outside (right-to-left, i.e. the $i$-th line of code must run before the $(i+1)$-th line of code),
this is not required for the chain rule \eqref{eq:ad}.
The Jacobian product is associative, so it can be evaluated in any order.
This property gives rise to two modes of automatic differentiation:
\begin{subequations}
\begin{align}
& \text{Forward-mode:} && \text{seed } \pdv{f_1}{p} = 1 \text{ and propagate } \pdv{f_i}{p} = J_i \cdot \pdv{f_{i-1}}{p} , \label{eq:ad_forward} \\
& \text{Reverse-mode:} && \text{seed } \pdv{f_N}{p} = 1 \text{ and propagate } \pdv{f_N}{p} = \pdv{f_{i+1}}{p} \cdot J_i . \label{eq:ad_backward}
\end{align}
\end{subequations}
The seeds only state that the derivative of the input or output with respect to itself is $1$.
They are mathematically equivalent, but differ fundamentally in how they are implemented and in performance characteristics.
A rule of thumb is that forward-mode is simpler to implement and performs better when there are many more outputs than inputs ($m \ll n$),
while reverse-mode is more challenging to implement and performs better when there are many more inputs than outputs ($m \gg n$).
SymBoltz currently only supports forward-mode, so it is what I will focus on here.
This most appropriate for taking derivatives of distances and power spectra at 100s-1000s of $\{z,k,l\}$
as a function of 1-10 cosmological parameters.
\TODO{but the same is not true for a scalar likelihood function}

\textbf{Dual numbers} are a natural way to implement forward-mode automatic differentiation.
Similarly to how a complex number $z = x + i y$ is a pair of real numbers $x$ and $y$ defined with an algebra such that $i^2 = -1$,
a dual number $z = x + \epsilon y$ is a pair of real numbers defined with an algebra such that $\epsilon^2 = 0$.
The significance of this is that $\epsilon^2 = \epsilon^3 = \ldots = 0$,
so the Taylor expansion to first order of any function $f(z)$ on dual numbers is \emph{exactly}
\begin{equation}
f(x + \epsilon y) = f(x) + \epsilon \, y f^\prime(y).
\end{equation}
This means that if $z = f_{i-1} + \epsilon f_{i-1}^\prime$ holds the value $f_{i-1}$ and derivative $f_{i-1}^\prime = \pdv{f_{i-1}}/{p}$ of the $i$-th line of code in the program \eqref{eq:program},
then $f_i(z) = f_i(f_{i-1}) + \epsilon f_{i-1}^\prime f_i^\prime(f_{i-1})$ holds the value and derivative at the $(i+1)$-th line by the forward chain rule \eqref{eq:ad_forward}.
By starting at $i=0$, evaluating the program with dual numbers accumulate both $F$ and $\pdv{F}/{P}$ at the final line $i=N$.

It requires that every elementary function $f_i$ supports both standard real numbers and dual numbers.
For example, the function $\sin(x)$ must be overloaded to return $\sin(x)$ when called with real $z$,
but $\sin(x) + \epsilon \, y \cos(x)$ when called with dual $z = x + \epsilon \, y$.
This is a problem in the space of programming language design and compiler architecture,
as they must allow any given function to be to different machine code for real numbers versus dual numbers.
In addition, it is not always known at compile-time which functions must be compiled in which ways,
as this differs for different sets of cosmological parameters that influence the computation in different ways.
For this reason, just-in-time (JIT) compiled languages and frameworks have arisen and become popular to accommodate this functionality.
This includes fully JIT-compiled programming languages (e.g. Julia, in which SymBoltz and Bolt is written),
and JIT \enquote{sublanguages} or frameworks inside interpreted languages (e.g. JAX in Python, in which DISCO-EB and ABCMB implemented).
Here the code is compiled only just before it is evaluated,
i.e. when it is known what must be compiled in different ways.
For this reason, it would be more challenging to support automatic differentiation in CAMB and CLASS,
which are writted in Fortran and C that are ahead-of-time (AOT) compiled programming languages.

\subsection{Exploiting smoothness with spectral methods}
\label{sec:spectral}

The absence of approximation schemes means that the same ODE is evolved at all times.
Consequently, the perturbation solution $u(\tau, k)$ is a \emph{smooth} function of both $\tau$ and $k$
(down to the numerical noise set by the ODE solver).
This is not true to the same extent with approximations,
as the transitions between equations at different $(\tau,k)$ (see \cref{fig:approximations})
can introduce small discontinuities or kinks in the solution.
This leads me to ask the following question:
\begin{displayquote}
Is it possible to exploit the smoothness of the perturbation solutions $u(\tau, k)$ due to approximation-freeness?
\end{displayquote}

\emph{Spectral} methods are a class of computational methods (e.g. for ODEs, interpolation and quadrature).
What they have in common is that they assume the underlying function (e.g. the ODE solution, interpolant or integrand) to be smooth.
Spectral methods make use of this to expand the function into a linear combination of \emph{global} basis functions that run over the whole computational domain.
This is in contrast to \emph{local} (non-spectral) methods that work with one subdomain at a time.
The major selling point of spectral methods is that they can converge exponentially fast (e.g. with error $\rho^{-N}$ for $N$ points and some $\rho>1$),
whereas local methods converge algebraically fast (e.g. as $N^{-p}$, where $p$ is usually the method's order of convergence).
However, if the function is not smooth, even if it only has a kink at one point,
then the exponential convergence is degraded and local methods are safer.
As \cref{tab:spectral} illustrates, approximation-free \EB{} codes open the door to such methods.

In fact, spectral methods are already used in some places in \EB{} codes.
For example, the multipole expansion \eqref{eq:decompose_legendre} is spectral on $\mu \in [-1, 1]$,
and the use of Gaussian quadrature for the $q$-integrals \eqref{eq:energy_momentum_integrals_bg} and \eqref{eq:energy_momentum_integrals_pert} is spectral on $q \in [0, \infty)$%
\footnote{COSMICS used 8th order Newton-Cotes quadrature (a local method) and needed 128 $q$-points \cite{maCosmologicalPerturbationTheory1995a},
while CLASS uses Gaussian quadrature (a spectral method) with only 5 $q$-points \cite{lesgourguesCosmicLinearAnisotropy2011f}!}.

In \cref{paper:chebyshev},
I show that approximation-free \EB{} codes can do the interpolation in $k$ and $l$ (see \cref{sec:eb_codes})
using global Chebyshev polynomials (a spectral method)%
\footnote{Interpolation with Fourier series is perhaps more familiar and is spectral for smooth and periodic functions.
Chebyshev interpolation is its closest analogue for smooth but non-periodic functions.}
instead of cubic splines (a local method, used by most other \EB{} codes).
Chebyshev interpolation is used both to interpolate the perturbation solution $u(\tau,k)$ in $k$ and angular power spectra $C_l$ in $l$,
so fewer perturbation $k$-modes \eqref{eq:pt_ode} and line-of-sight integrals \eqref{eq:los} need to be solved for unchanged precision.
This reinforces the position of approximation-free \EB{} codes:
instead of using model-dependent approximation schemes that complicate the physics,
we can relax approximation schemes and even \enquote{win back} some of the performance that is sacrificed in the process
by taking advantage of the smoothness of the resulting solution.

In my opinion, the improvement is not revolutionary given the $\approx 0.1\%$ precision target for current cosmological surveys,
where interpolation with cubic splines is not much worse.
However, as future surveys require even greater precision from our modeling tools, I believe that spectral methods will become more important.
In theory, going from $0.1\%$ to $0.01\%$ precision requires increasing $N$ to $10^{1/p} \times N$ with algebraic convergence,
but only to $N + \log(10)/\log(\rho)$ with exponential convergence.

This is not the only way spectral methods may take advantage of smoothness.
For example, several works have improved the computation of angular power spectra to relax the Limber approximation by using spectral methods

\chapter{The nonlinear part}
\label{chap:nonlin}

When gravity acts sufficiently long on matter,
the assumption $\delta \ll 1$ of linear perturbation theory in \cref{chap:lin} sooner or later breaks down.
To accurately describe large-scale structure (LSS) at small scales (high $k \gtrsim 0.1\,h/\mathrm{Mpc}$) in the late universe (for low $z \lesssim 10$),
we must incorporate nonlinear effects into our modeling.
This makes nonlinear effects important for late-time cosmological probes,
such as galaxy clustering and weak lensing.

$N$-body simulations is a fundamental method for doing this.
They simulate a great number of particles that represent the clustering of cold dark matter (CDM) in the universe.
However, they are much more computationally expensive than \EB{} codes that solve linear perturbations.
This makes it particularly demanding to use them to explore the parameter space of the cosmological model,
and even more so when considering modified models.

\Cref{paper:bransdicke} in this thesis presents a method for computing the \enquote{boost}
\begin{equation}
B(z, k/h; \mathbf{p}) = \frac{P_\mathrm{BD}(z,k/h; \mathbf{p})}{P_\mathrm{GR}(z,k/h; \mathbf{p})}
\label{eq:boost_intro}
\end{equation}
of the nonlinear matter power spectra $P(z,k)$ \BD{} gravity
-- a modified theory of gravity --
compared to \GR{} as a function of cosmological parameters $\mathbf{p}$.
This is achieved by running a cheap form of $N$-body simulations using the COLA method
in order to probe parameter space dependence.
The paper shows how one may exploit the freedom of parameterization of the boost \eqref{eq:boost_intro}
in a way that makes it possible to compute it solely using linear theory and \EB{} codes from \cref{chap:lin}.

\section{The need for $N$-body simulations}

At late times and small scales (high $k$),
the anisotropic stress \eqref{eq:pertein3} is negligible and we may set $\phi = \psi$.
In equation \eqref{eq:pertein1},
it is then the first term that dominates,
so we may write it as a Poisson equation:
\begin{equation}
\nabla^2 \psi(\tau,\mathbf{x}) = 4\pi a(\tau)^2 \delta\rho_m(\tau,\mathbf{x}) .
\label{eq:poisson_gr}
\end{equation}
In the nonlinear regime, the growth of matter breaks the assumption $\delta_m \ll 1$ of linear theory (i.e. in the Boltzmann equations),
but we may continue to treat the metric perturbation to as linear perturbations (i.e. the Einstein equations)
because the Poisson equation \eqref{eq:poisson_gr} suppresses it by a factor $k^{-2}$.

One way to fix the breakdown of the linear assumption $\delta \ll 1$,
which is valid up to $k \lesssim 0.1\,h/\mathrm{Mpc}$ or so,
is to take the perturbation theory in \cref{chap:lin} to higher orders.
This can incorporate nonlinear effects up to $k \lesssim 0.3\,h/\mathrm{Mpc}$ \TODO{cite Dodelson}.

However, a deeper problem that not even higher-order perturbation theory can resolve properly
is the \emph{shell crossing} phenomenon.
Consider a spherically symmetric CDM distribution that collapses under gravity.
A shell that starts at some sufficiently small radius fall in,
pass unimpeded through the origin (because CDM is collisionless),
and on its way out meet a shell that started at a higher radius and is still infalling.
At the time $\tau$ and position $\mathbf{x}$ where they meet,
the velocity field $\mathbf{u}(\tau, \mathbf{x})$ becomes multivalued.
Perturbation theory (linear or higher-order) uses a fluid description (as in \cref{sec:fluid}),
which mathematically assumes a well-defined velocity field $\mathbf{u}(\tau, \mathbf{x})$ at all $\tau$ and $\mathbf{x}$.
This fundamental assumption breaks down due to shell crossing.

$N$-body simulations handle this by evolving the \enquote{microscopic} distribution function $f$ of CDM particles (from \cref{sec:boltzmann}),
instead of its \enquote{macroscopic} fluid variables such as $\delta$.
The idea is to spawn particles with positions $\mathbf{x}_i$ and momenta $\mathbf{p}_i$
that sample the distribution function in phase space,
and evolve each of them under the geodesic equation.
Each particle accelerates due to the (gradient of) the potential $\psi(\tau,\mathbf{x})$.
Formally, this leads to a system of equations called the Vlasov--Poisson equation,
where the Poisson equation \eqref{eq:poisson_gr} is coupled to a non-relativistic and collisionless Boltzmann equation,
which is called the Vlasov equation.
\TODO{give Dodelson eq 12.54?}.

Initial conditions for $N$-body simulations are provided from linear theory.
It is standard to arrange the initial particles such that
they realize a linear matter power spectrum that is valid at a sufficiently early times
before linearity breaks down and shell crossing is a problem.
Thus, a modified theory in an $N$-body code also needs a corresponding implementation in an \EB{} code.
Furthermore, $N$-body simulations usually only account for cold dark matter.
To incorporate the effects of baryons, massive neutrinos, dark energy perturbations and other things that are not in the $N$-body code,
it is common to evolve the \EB{} code until today and output its matter power spectrum,
and then \enquote{backscale} using linear growth factors to the start of the $N$-body simulation \TODO{cite backscaling IC paper}.
They are thus far from independent on the linear theory.

Unfortunately, standard $N$-body simulations are very expensive.
Accurate results can require millions to billions of particles advected over thousands of time steps.
This makes it problematic to use them for exploring parameter space and even more so when exploring modified models.

Fortunately, there is a clever way of alleviating this issue called the \textbf{COmoving Lagrangian Acceleration} (COLA) \textbf{method} \cite{tassevSolvingLargeScale2013b}.
It is based on the insight that at large scales (low $k$),
$N$-body simulations spend most of their effort on computing simple streaming-like particle motion by brute force.
This can be well described using analytical approximations with the framework of Lagrangian Perturbation Theory (LPT) \TODO{ref?}.
The COLA method modifies the standard $N$-body method by not solving directly for the geodesic trajectory $\mathbf{x}$ of each particle,
but instead only for residual from LPT to, say, second order:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{x}_\mathrm{res} = \mathbf{x} - \mathbf{x}_\mathrm{LPT}^{(2)} .
\label{eq:cola}
\end{equation}
Here $\mathbf{x}_\mathrm{LPT}$ is the approximation to $\mathbf{x}$ that is valid to,
say, second order in LPT, and is constructed by growth factors for the theory \TODO{true?}.
This trick lets COLA simulations sacrifice some accuracy at the very smallest scales
(where uncertainties of baryonic physics enter anyway),
in order to greatly reduce the number of time steps necessary.
This makes COLA $N$-body simulations suitable for probing parameter space
including nonlinear effects up to mildly nonlinear scales $k \lesssim 1\,h/\mathrm{Mpc}$.
Furthermore, the sacrifice of small-scale accuracy is made less severe
and pairs very well with computing the boost \eqref{eq:boost_error},
where the error cancels to some extent because it is made both in \BD{} and \GR{}.

\section{\BD{} modified gravity}

\BD{} gravity is a Horndeski theory of modified gravity,
with the spacetime metric $g_{\mu\nu}$ and a scalar field $\varphi$ as degrees of freedom
and the following action:
\begin{equation}
S[g_{\mu\nu}, \varphi] = \frac{1}{16\pi} \int \diff^4 x \sqrt{-\lvert g \rvert} \left( \varphi R - \frac{\omega}{\varphi} (\nabla \varphi)^2 \right) + S_M .
\end{equation}
The scalar field is coupled to the Ricci scalar
and has a kinetic term proportional to the \BD{} coupling parameter (which is time-independent).

By variation of the action $\delta S/\delta_{g^{\mu\nu}} = \delta S/\delta \varphi = 0$,
the equations of motion are:
\begin{subequations}
\label{eq:bd_eom}
\begin{align}
G_{\mu\nu} &= \frac{8\pi}{\varphi} T_{\mu\nu}
+ \frac{\omega}{\varphi^2} \Big( \nabla_\mu \varphi \nabla_\nu \varphi - \frac12 g_{\mu\nu} (\nabla \varphi)^2 \Big)
+ \frac{1}{\varphi} \Big( \nabla_\mu \nabla_\nu \varphi - g_{\mu\nu} \nabla^2 \varphi \Big) , \label{eq:bd_eom1} \\
\nabla^2 \varphi &= \frac{8\pi}{3+2\omega} T , \label{eq:bd_eom2}
\end{align}
\end{subequations}
where $(\nabla \varphi)^2 = g^{\mu\nu} \, \nabla_\mu \varphi \, \nabla_\nu \varphi$,
$\nabla^2 = g^{\mu\nu} \, \nabla_\mu \nabla_\nu$,
and $T = T^\mu_\mu = g^{\mu\nu} \, T_{\mu\nu}$
is the trace of the energy-momentum tensor $T_{\mu\nu}$ of the matter action $S_M$.
\GR{} and the Einstein equations \eqref{eq:einstein} are recovered in the limit $\omega \rightarrow \infty$ with $\varphi = 1$.

Using the perturbed FLRW metric \eqref{eq:flrw_perturbed},
one can derive the corresponding background and perturbation equations and implement them in an \EB{} code (as in \cref{chap:lin}).
The Poisson equation \eqref{eq:poisson_gr} in the matter-dominated late universe (necessary for the $N$-body implementation) becomes:
\begin{equation}
\nabla^2 \psi(\tau, \mathbf{x}) = 4\pi \mu(\tau) a(\tau)^2 \delta\rho_m(\tau,\mathbf{x}),
\qquad
\mu(\tau) = \frac{2\omega+4}{2\omega+3} \frac{1}{\bar\varphi(\tau)} .
\label{eq:poisson_bd}
\end{equation}
It shows that \BD{} gravity effectively promotes Newton's constant $\mu=1$ in \GR{}
to a time-dependent gravitational coupling $\mu(\tau)$.

Why are we interested in \BD{}, in particular?
In fact, \BD{} might appear as a non-viable modification of gravity,
as Table 1 in \cref{paper:bransdicke} shows that $\omega$ has been constrained to increasingly higher values over time.
The first point is that a subset of all Horndeski theories of modified gravity
reduce to \BD{} on cosmological distance scales.
Thus, constraints on its validity can rather be interpreted as deviations from \GR{}
(parameterized in the direction of Horndeski theories in \enquote{model space})
\cite{avilezCosmologicalConstraintsBransDicke2014b,joudakiTestingGravityCosmic2022a}.
The second point (also in Table 1) is that constraints on \BD{} fall in two categories:
those from small-scale experiments (e.g. solar system tests and Pulsar/Shapiro timing) and large-scale probes (e.g. cosmological observables such as CMB/BAO/LSS).
As the table shows, small-scale experiments have historically been tighter,
so \BD{} offers a way of benchmarking the strength of the constraints versus cosmological constraints.
The third point is to illustrate a new strategy for
computing the nonlinear corrections in a cheap way by mapping it back to results from linear theory.
This strategy is explained in the next section,
and could also be used for incorporating nonlinearities from other modified theories and models.

\section{Emulating the matter power spectrum boost}
\label{sec:boost}

The goal of \cref{paper:bransdicke} is to compute the nonlinear matter power spectrum \eqref{eq:boost_intro}
in a way that is sufficiently accurate, simple and fast.
It may then be combined with existing emulators for $P_\mathrm{GR}$,
such as EuclidEmulator2 \TODO{cite} (which are carefully designed since GR is the fiducial model),
which is trained on the output of many $N$-body simulations,
in order to predict the full \BD{} spectrum $P_\mathrm{BD} = B \cdot P_\mathrm{GR}$ in a cheap way.

Several papers do this for other modified gravity theories \TODO{cite examples}.
They do this by creating new emulators for the boost $B$ trained on the output of several $N$-body simulations,
using neural networks or similar function approximation methods.

First and foremost, approximating $B = P_\mathrm{BD}/P_\mathrm{GR}$ instead of $P_\mathrm{BD}$
is a fundamentally easier task because any error that we make in both $P_\mathrm{BD}$ and $P_\mathrm{GR}$ will partially cancel:
\begin{equation}
B = \frac{P_\mathrm{BD}}{P_{\mathrm{GR}}} = 1 + \frac{P_\mathrm{BD}-P_\mathrm{GR}}{P_{\mathrm{GR}}} .
\label{eq:boost_error}
\end{equation}

Second, our strategy exploits the freedom of parameterization of the boost:
\begin{equation}
B(z, k/h; \mathbf{p}_\mathrm{BD}) = \frac{P_\mathrm{BD}(z, k/h; \mathbf{p}_\mathrm{BD})}{P_\mathrm{GR}(z, k/h; \mathbf{p}_\mathrm{GR}(\mathbf{p}_\mathrm{BD}))}
\end{equation}
The point is that we are free to define \emph{how} we compare a \GR{} universe to a \BD{} universe.
We first run a \BD{} simulation with parameters $\mathbf{p}_\mathrm{BD}$,
then compute the parameters of a corresponding \GR{} universe
with a transformation $\mathbf{p}_\mathrm{GR}(\mathbf{p}_\mathbf{BD})$%
\footnote{In principle we could also make
$z_\mathrm{GR}(z_\mathrm{BD})$ and $k_\mathrm{GR}(k_\mathrm{BD})$.
The notation $P(z, k/h)$ means that we compare spectra at equal $z$ and $k/h$
(but different $k$, as we define $\mathbf{p}_\mathrm{GR}(\mathbf{p}_\mathrm{BD})$ with $h_\mathrm{GR} \neq h_\mathrm{BD}$).}
of \emph{our own choosing},
and finally run the \GR{} simulation.
The overarching point is that we can take advantage of this freedom to \enquote{design}
a boost function that is as simple as possible to emulate.%
\footnote{To make the point clearly:
In the extreme case, suppose that we could find a parameter transformation
$\mathbf{p}_\mathrm{GR}(\mathbf{p}_\mathbf{BD})$ such that $B = 1$ exactly.
That creates a trivial mapping for computing $P(z, k; \mathbf{p}_\mathbf{BD})$ exactly.
}

What we find in \cref{paper:bransdicke} is that
if we use the value of $\sigma_8$, $\varphi_\ini h^2$, $\rho_{m0}$ and $\rho_{r0}$ in \GR{} and \BD{},
and we compare spectra at equal $z$ and $k/h$;
then the boost of the \emph{nonlinear} spectra (from $N$-body simulations)
very closely (to $1$-$2\,\%$ match the boost of the \emph{linear} spectra (from \EB{} codes).
We show this by running a few $N$-body simulations and comparing the boosts to those from \EB{} codes.

Thus, instead of going through the trouble of running many expensive $N$-body simulations or training emulator from a suite of them,
we can compute the boost efficiently by only two calls to cheap \EB{} solvers.

\section{Outlook}

The emulation strategy described in \cref{paper:bransdicke} and \cref{sec:boost}
could be used to create simple and fast ways to constrain other modified theories of gravity, too.
By starting with one carefully designed emulator of $P_\mathrm{GR}(z, k)$ for \GR{},
one could in this way build up a network of \enquote{boost approximations} (or emulators)
that predicts nonlinear matter power spectra for several modified gravity models.

\subsection{AI/LLM usage}

In a thesis about numerical methods, coding and software in astronomy in 2026,
I cannot avoid discussing the impact of artificial intelligence (AI) and large language models (LLMs) on software development.
ChatGPT was launched by OpenAI -- an extremely misleading name -- in November 2022, only a few months after I began my PhD.
This sparked a race among competitors, and Microsoft/Google/Antrophic launched Copilot/Gemini/Claude the next year.
These chatbot services were eagerly tested and adopted by many people in personal usage, scientific workcases and industry workplaces.

However, in 2025 and onwards, these services have evolved further into \emph{agentic} AI systems.
They have access to a computer, can read and write code and files, run programs, analyze results and inspect error messages.
This ability addresses the hallucination problem of AI, since now a model can iteratively correct itself when it writes code that does not run or when it computes results that are not correct.
Copy-pasting the output of the chatbots.

Pressure to use these tools to increase productivity.
In my experience, a significant amount of PhD students even pay for these tools out of their own pockets,
while the university does currently not pay for it.

The increasing use of AI tools has sparked a debate in the scientific community about whether or not one should use them \TODO{refs}.
Created significant polarization in the community, regarding what is accepted and not.
Here I also offer my thoughts on the role of AI in scientific research and astronomy.

CLAX and nanoCMB?

Fallacies (in my opinion) about AI and LLMs:

LLMs are knowledge banks that are genuinely useful to navigate the literature.

Some people object that they do not have knowledge or intelligence, but that they are only statistical machines for predicting which word comes after another word.
But there is a correlation between words/language and knowledge.

Good computing is about finding the right level of abstraction.
AI can be viewed as another layer of abstraction, that allows humans to focus on high-level tasks merely by writing textual prompts,
while agentic AI performs lower-level tasks.
It is inevitable that as the volume of human knowledge and \enquote{the literature} grows,
finding ways to \enquote{compress} or accelerate the existing knowledge will become more important,
unless we want children to spend an increasing part of their lives in elementary school.

Short prompts and long output are a symptom of a problem:
the abstraction (say software library) does not exist to handle the prompt,
OR we are for some reason motivated to produce e.g. a 12-page research article about a 
Thus, I believe that journals have an important role in modernizing themselves,
for example in making it possible to publish research findings in other formats that allow for smaller \enquote{chunks} (e.g. using HTML)
rather than forcing everything through the (sometimes bureaucratic).
For example, in mathematics they have started using \enquote{graph networks} with knowledge nodes.

The training problem: AI needs to be verified, but if noone does things manually anymore, then noone will be able to verify.

Another problem: \enquote{bad}/\enquote{messy} software engineering practices in sciences amplifies the problem.
Some people treat scripting only as a means to an end to produce scientific results:
it can be however messy you want, and all you care about is the input and output.
This workflow is what attracts the use of AI/LLMs.
Instead, I believe we should take the software engineering aspect in science seriously,
and strive to create modular and well-tested libraries and package that can build upon eachother.

Others dismiss AI for not being sustainable due to massive use of energy, and that the AI companies are subsidizing usage in early phases and that the cost will ramp up significantly in the future.
But as with any technology ever created, I believe it is a failure to not account for that the technology will improve and become more efficient in the future.

In my opinion, communicating research results should always be done by humans.

I think it is genuinely very important that we, as the cosmological research community,
resolve the \enquote{political tension} in AI use and figure out the accepted way to use these tools while preserving the literature that we want as humans.
