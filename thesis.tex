\section{The standard model of cosmology}

Over the last century, the standard model of cosmology has been successively refined and evolved to the standard \LCDM{} model.
Here the cosmological constant ($\Lambda$) acts as dark energy,
and cold dark matter (CDM) is a collisionless form of matter.

With the detection of the cosmic microwave background (CMB) in the 1960s,
the earlier steady state model was put to rest.
It argued that the expansion of the universe is eternal and that it satisfies a theoretically elegant \emph{perfect cosmological principle},
where the universe is homogeneous and isotropic in both space and time.
In other words, the universe should look the same also when looking backward and forward in time.
It also argues that the background microwave radiation was due to starlight being scattered by galactic dust.
Penzias and Wilson measured the microwave background with a finite temperature in 1964,
whose values was consistent with that of a perfect black body but redshifted from an early state with a hot and dense state that we call the \emph{Big Bang} today.

In 1990, COBE confirmed that the spectrum of the microwave background matched that of a black body to unprecedented precision.
In following years, WMAP and Planck further refined observations of what was now referred to as the \emph{cosmic} microwave background.
This made it possible to shift from decades of research on the \emph{qualitative} features of the universe
into \emph{quantitative} research on precision cosmology, measuring parameters of cosmological models.

\subsection{General Relativistic gravity}

In general relativity, space and time are geometrically intertwined on a $3+1$-dimensional manifold,
whose fundamental object is the metric tensor $g_{\mu\nu}(x)$.
The most fundamental way of formulating it is as a field theory with the Einstein--Hilbert action
\begin{equation}
    S = \frac{1}{16\pi} \int \diff{^4 x} \sqrt{-g} R,
\end{equation}
where $g<0$ is the determinant of the metric and the Ricci scalar $R$ is the simplest scalar invariant
(invariant to coordinate transformations) that describes the curvature of spacetime.

The equations of motion follow from the principle of least action:
varying the action $S$ with respect to the metric $g_{\mu\nu}(x)$ gives the Einstein field equations:
\begin{equation}
    G_{\mu\nu} + \Lambda g_{\mu\nu} = 8\pi T_{\mu\nu}.
\end{equation}
Here $G_{\mu\nu} = R_{\mu\nu} - \frac12 g_{\mu\nu} R$ is the Einstein tensor,
$R_{\mu\nu}$ is the Ricci tensor and $R$ \TODO{tensor notation package} the Ricci scalar.
The left side of the equation is purely \emph{geometrical} and a function of the metric $g_{\mu\nu}$ alone.
The right side of the equation contains the stress-energy tensor $T_{\mu\nu}$, which encodes the density and flux of energy due to the \emph{matter} content that fills space and time.
It is in this way that \enquote{spacetime tells matter how to move; matter tells spacetime how to curve} \TODO{ref Wheeler}.

From the Einstein field equations, it follows that the conservation of energy and momentum
\begin{equation}
    \nabla_\mu T^{\mu\nu} = 0.
\end{equation}

\subsection{Dark matter}

Using velocity measurements of stars in galaxies, or of galaxies in galaxy clusters,
Lord Kelvin, Jacobus Kapteyn and Fritz Zwicky independently (?) suggested that the universe contains more matter than the luminous matter we can observe from Earth.
At the time it was thought that the missing matter was ordinary matter that was simply hard to observe due to it being very dim.
In 50 years, evidence piled up that suggested that dark matter is a type of matter that behaves qualitatively differently from ordinary matter:
\enquote{extinguished stars, dark clouds, meteors, comets, and so on} \TODO{ref}. % https://arxiv.org/pdf/1605.04909

In the 1970s, observations of galaxy rotation curves added more weight to the dark matter hypothesis.
Assuming a Newtonian gravitational force $F = g m M / r^2$ and centripetal acceleration $a = v^2/r$,
one can easily use measurements of $v$ to compute the mass $M(r) = v^2 r / G$ enclosed within $r$
and in turn the density $\rho(r) = \diff{M} / 4\pi r^2 \diff{r}$.
In the outskirts of a halo, beyond which there is no visible matter,
one would expect that $M(r)$ is constant so $v(r) \propto r^{-1/2}$ should decay.
However, observations instead show $v(r) \propto \const$.
This discrepancy is now widely thought to be due to a halo of dark matter that surrounds galaxies like a cloud.

\subsection{Linear \EB{} codes}

Linear \EB{} codes solve the background and linear perturbations of the \EB{} equations that define a cosmological model.
This approach is accurate for large distance scales, up to $k \lesssim 0.1 \, h/\Mpc$,
but on shorter scales one must resort to higher-order (and more complicated) perturbation theory or nonlinear simulations.
For example, they predict and output distance measures and power spectra for the distribution of matter and the CMB.

Generally, this is the must fundamental code that must be modified for a given cosmological model,
requiring the implementation of background (including thermodynamcis) and perturbation equations.
These codes stitch together a wide range of advanced numerical methods to solve several steps.
For every set of new cosmological parameters, the codes solve large and stiff ODEs, evaluate thousands of integral equations, and compute special functions such as (hyper)spherical Bessel functions.
Despite all these challenging steps, these codes have been refined and optimized with approximation schemes over several decades,
and now run on the order of seconds on a normal laptop.

As of today, the two most established codes in the community are CAMB \cite{lewisEfficientComputationCMB2000a} and CLASS \cite{lesgourguesCosmicLinearAnisotropy2011d,blasCosmicLinearAnisotropy2011c}.
In \cref{chap:lin} I present a new \EB{} code called SymBoltz that aims to improve certain aspects of the traditional codes,
particularly related to the simplicity of specifying models, avoiding complicating approximation schemes and enabling automatic differentiation.

\cite{sletmoenSymBoltzjlSymbolicnumericApproximationfree2026}

\subsection{Emulators}

Linear \EB{} solvers, and to a greater extent $N$-body simulations, are expensive to run.
However, applications such as bayesian parameter inference with MCMCs typically need their predictions at many points very quickly.
Recently very common to \emph{emulate} the output of expensive codes as a function of the cosmological parameters.

In full generality, \emph{emulation} is any method that uses a fast and approximate surrogate model to reproduce the output of an expensive function in a much cheaper way.

equations are defined with sympy,
then compiled to efficient C/C++ code using the sympy2c utility \cite{schmittSympy2cSymbolicExpressions2022a} that was made in tandem with the code.
It is also the first code to be truly approximation-free:
using the implicit BDF2 method coupled with the sparse and analytical Jacobians to completely circumvent approximation schemes found in older codes.

\item \textbf{SymBoltz} \cite{sletmoenSymBoltzjlSymbolicnumericApproximationfree2026}
is the first \EB{} code that is both symbolic-numeric, approximation-free and differentiable.

As a computational physicist,
a significant source of my interest in \EB{} codes come from their combination of a surprising number of numerical methods and algorithms:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Ordinary differential equations (ODEs):}
The most fundamental part of any \EB{} codes is the solution of ODEs in time $t$
\begin{equation}
    \frac{\diff u}{\diff t} = f(u, t, p),
    \quad
    u(t_\ini) = u_\ini.
\end{equation}
for the solution $u(t)$ of the background and perturbation equations $f(u, t, p)$ with initial conditions $u_\ini$ and parameters $p$.
The background (including thermodynamics\footnote{Many places the thermodynamics is not considered part of the background.}) typically features only 1-10 coupled equations,
while the perturbations feature 10s or 100s of coupled equations that must be solved repeatedly for 100s to 1000s of different values of the perturbation wavenumber $k \in p$.
Solving the equations is made even more challenging because they are \textbf{stiff} due to several different timescales coexisting in the same equations.
Standard explicit ODE methods,
such as the well-known Euler, RK4 and Dormand--Prince methods,
fail catastrophically at solving stiff ODEs by taking tiny step sizes and diverging with huge errors.
Some \EB{} codes remove the stiffness in the equations by approximating them with different versions in different regimes,
which makes the use of explicit solvers possible, but makes highly model-dependent statements that make it much more difficult to modify the model.
Instead of approximating the equations, one can solve the full stiff equations with implicit ODE solvers,
such as lesser-known BDF, Radau and Rosenbrock methods,
which are much more computationally demanding, harder to implement efficiently and has performance characteristics that depend much more on the equations than for explicit methods.

\item \textbf{Quadrature (numerical integration):}
With the introduction of the line-of-sight integration method by \cite{seljakLineSightApproach1996},
\EB{} codes switched to evaluating $\ell$-dependent photon multipoles from ODEs to integrals
\begin{equation}
    I(a, b) = \int_a^b f(t) \, \diff t.
\end{equation}
Every integral can be reduced to a one-dimensional ODE and thus solved with an ODE solver,
but most ODEs cannot be reformulated to integral equations.
Quadrature methods are methods designed specifically for evaluating full integrals without obtaining the cumulative solution at intermediate time points.

This workflow is what attracts the use of AI/LLMs.
Instead, I believe we should take the software engineering aspect in science seriously,
and strive to create modular and well-tested libraries and package that can build upon eachother.

Others dismiss AI for not being sustainable due to massive use of energy, and that the AI companies are subsidizing usage in early phases and that the cost will ramp up significantly in the future.
But as with any technology ever created, I believe it is a failure to not account for that the technology will improve and become more efficient in the future.

In my opinion, communicating research results should always be done by humans.

I think it is genuinely very important that we, as the cosmological research community,
resolve the \enquote{political tension} in AI use and figure out the accepted way to use these tools while preserving the literature that we want as humans.
